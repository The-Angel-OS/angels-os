Autobiography: Book 1
Chapter 1: Beginnings
I’ve read hundreds of books across every genre—biographies, novels, treatises, and memoirs. One thing I’ve noticed is that many autobiographies of so-called notable individuals often condense or skip over their childhoods entirely, reducing their early years to something like the Earth’s entry in The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy: “Mostly harmless.”
That’s not the approach I’m taking here.
What follows is as much of my life as I can remember, laid out not just for posterity, but as a beacon—on the off chance that my future, species-differentiated progeny might seek me out. And if not, then at the very least I’ve created a durable memory dump—something I can refer to as I try to understand who I am, what questions I should be asking, and maybe even receive the answers beamed to me during a meditation session. After all, isn’t that what we’re all doing here—trying to ask the right questions?
I’m increasingly convinced that we’re not just meat and motion. I believe consciousness is the underlying field. Our bodies are the gear—VR rigs for a soul playing a round in this simulation, like Ready Player One. The kicker is, you don’t get to take the headset off until it says “Game Over.”
In this incarnation, on this timeline, I want to continue living a meaningful life—on my terms—for as long as I choose. That, to me, is a goal worth writing for.
On Forgiveness and Family
Before I dive too deep, a few caveats.
First: I dearly loved my mother. We were affectionate, close, and I have vivid memories of our time together. But there are truths that shouldn’t be glossed over. I've come to believe that forgiveness is letting go—not forgetting, but surrendering the expectation that the past could’ve been any different. Let reality unfold as it will. So, when you read what follows, know this: I loved her then. I love her now.
Second: My stepfather. I’ve shared difficult memories involving him, but let me be unequivocally clear—I’m grateful. The truth is, I wouldn’t be the man I am without his strong (and often energetic) involvement in my formative years. I love my stepdad, and I’m glad we still have each other.
And third: My daughter. One day, she may write her own story, and if she does, I hope she tells it without apology—just as I’ve done here. She was yanked from a relatively stable, middle-class upbringing and thrown into chaos. She was humiliated, burdened with responsibilities no child should bear, and expected to mother her own mother. And yet... she rose. Lauren was tempered by that fire—not broken. She’s one of the most resilient, wise, grounded people I’ve ever known. I am endlessly proud of her.
Lastly: I love nearly everyone I’m writing about here. Even the guy who swerved into my lane this morning on U.S. 19. I can’t quite say I love everyone yet, but I’m aware that forgiveness—even for people like that—is part of the path.
The First Breath
So, with that out of the way: I was born on a frigid January day in 1972, on the 15th, at George Washington Hospital Center. That date would later be dedicated to the memory of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.—a man whose ideals would become integral to my worldview. Knowing I was born on his birthday always gave me a quiet sense of purpose.
I grew up in Bowie, Maryland, a town of suburban charm nestled between Washington D.C. and Annapolis. My brother, three years older, was my first fraternal bond. At age five, I also gained a stepsister, two years my junior. Together we formed a triangle of sibling energy—sometimes harmonious, sometimes turbulent.
My earliest memory is lying in my crib, staring up at a twirling mobile. The house was alive with the sounds of Redskins football on Sundays, my father’s cheers echoing from the TV room. I remember sprinting through the house naked like it was nothing—a free little spirit in a home that felt safe enough for that.
Roots and Routines
Both of my parents graduated from the University of Maryland. My father worked at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center while earning his master’s from George Washington University. He was the real deal—calm, responsible, sharp.
My mom was a nurse—at first, in maternity and elder care, then later in medical weight loss. Like many in our family, she struggled with her weight. That challenge would echo through generations, affecting her health and her mind.
Because mental illness also ran through our maternal line. Her mother suffered, and she did too. It was an unspoken shadow that hung over our home—a silent architect of many life events to come.
The First Fracture
The unity between my parents eventually fractured under the weight of that shadow. When I was just three, my mother left. I didn’t understand it then. I only knew that something was missing. Her absence was the first big shift—the first ripple in the water that would later become a wave.
When I was fourteen, she passed away. Her death marked a permanent line in my emotional timeline. Childhood ended. And the normal I thought I knew—that ideal I’d quietly been chasing—was gone.
But here's what I know now: normal is a fiction. A momentary stillness. Life, like consciousness, is in motion.
And so am I.
Chapter 2: Formative Threads
In the suburbs of Bowie, Maryland, my world unfolded within the rhythms of a typical, moderately privileged middle-class American childhood. Whitehall was a neighborhood woven from familiar routines, Redskins fandom, and shared summer escapes at the Whitehall Pool and Tennis Club. It was a time when football games created a communal pulse, and the cheering on Sundays served as a kind of neighborhood liturgy.
The Washington Redskins were a cultural cornerstone in our household. My father lived and breathed the game, and by extension, so did I. Although my passions would eventually veer toward other pursuits—like programming, skating, dogs, and pondering the Answer to Life, the Universe, and Everything—my early wardrobe was practically sponsored by the Redskins: jackets, gloves, stocking caps, all branded in burgundy and gold. His favorite teams were the Redskins—and whoever was playing the Dallas Cowboys.
Yet even amid that warm suburban embrace, there were long, quiet gaps. My mother would disappear without explanation for hours at a time. The silence that followed wasn’t just empty—it was palpable. These absences were an early lesson in uncertainty, in the fragility of presence, and the ways reality can shift without notice.
Two Sets of Grandparents, Two Different Worlds
Throughout my early life, I spent a great deal of time with both my paternal and maternal grandparents. It was a blessing, especially after 1976, when my maternal grandparents—the Vassies—moved to Palm Harbor, Florida. This gave me an annual retreat from my parents’ constant conflicts and offered the calm and warmth of a place run by the world’s most gracious gentleman: my grandfather, Harry B. Vassie.
Before their move, while still living in College Park, the Vassies owned a 26-foot Chris Craft moored at Kent Island Marina. We’d cruise to Annapolis and other historic destinations on the Chesapeake Bay, eating crab cakes and basking in the serenity of the water. These experiences would forever shape my love for maritime life and, ultimately, my decision to enlist in the Navy. My grandfather, a decorated officer in U.S. Naval Intelligence, served with distinction before becoming part of the team that helped establish the NSA in the aftermath of WWII.
In contrast, my grandparents Courtney lived in Greenbelt and offered their own gifts. My grandmother Courtney embodied pure agape love. Time with her was magic. Saturdays at their house meant trips to the Laurel and Bowie racetracks, long hours of waiting between races while my grandfather studied his trifectas, and packed lunches eaten in smoky grandstands. I didn’t always appreciate it then, but I do now.
My brother and I would wander the woods around the NOAA headquarters, making our way to the Greenbelt airport, home to an incredible model airplane park. We’d watch, mesmerized, as mini aircraft looped and dived—sometimes crashing in spectacular fashion. These outings were also how we spent time with my Uncle Paul, the youngest of my grandparents’ children. I suspect that to some, my life path might have mirrored his. But I see the difference, and I’ve long forgiven those who couldn’t.
Guardians and Daycare Saints
My early years were also shaped by a rotating cast of caregivers. The Gonya family, close friends of my brother, left a lifelong imprint. Then came the Colantonis, with whom I spent a few formative years. We’ve since lost touch, but at the time, they felt like extended family. It was during this phase that I experienced one of life’s true cinematic moments—watching Star Wars for the first time in 1977, sitting next to the Drogamirer boys, a moment that forever etched a galaxy far, far away into my psyche.
Eventually, the Gonyas and Colantonis moved on. In fourth and fifth grade, I ended up with a caregiver whose name has faded from memory. Her backyard boasted an epic sandbox—an endless battlefield for my GI Joes. After school, we watched Star Blazers, a Japanese anime that awakened something deep and imaginative in me. Still, the experience wasn’t quite the same. Unlike earlier daycare settings that felt like family, this one had boundaries—rooms I couldn’t enter, spaces off-limits. It was my first taste of structured exclusion. Not hostile, just different. That phase ended when I became a latchkey kid.
Learning Grounds and Living History
My academic life began at Tom Thumb Nursery School, a place that felt like a fantasy realm. The playground was enormous, dotted with child-sized buildings and open fields—an ideal setting for unrestrained creativity. We climbed, imagined, experimented, and yes, occasionally got hurt. That was the point. Looking back, I doubt today’s world would permit what we did there. And that’s a loss. We were learning risk, boundaries, and the opposite sex, all without supervision or lawsuits.
From there, I went on to Meadowbrook Elementary, a school emblematic of a society in transition. I was part of the first wave of Generation X—the generation that witnessed the closing of Meadowbrook due to a shrinking student population, even as desegregation brought more diversity into our classrooms. I remember the bus routes changing, the faces in the hallway becoming more colorful. It was beautiful. The lessons of inclusivity weren’t taught—they were lived.
Across the street, Whitehall Elementary stood shuttered, another relic of the Baby Boom's demographic hangover. Later, it would find new life, but during my childhood, it was a silent monument to generational shift.
After Meadowbrook, I attended Rockledge Elementary from third through sixth grade. Every day I walked 0.9 miles to and from school—a trip filled with morning frost, Halloween-costumed parades of classmates, and the occasional detour. These treks were adventures, not chores. Life then still had a physicality to it. You moved through the world, not just your screen.
Living so close to the nation's capital also brought unique privileges. Museums, monuments, field trips to the Smithsonian, the Air and Space Museum, the Capitol Building—all were just a short ride away. My education didn’t end in the classroom. The history of a nation, its victories and flaws, was a living backdrop to my growth.
Woven Threads of Self
In every season, with every caregiver, every classroom, and every track day with my grandfather, something essential was being knit together: a curious, restless, fiercely observant kid who was just beginning to realize that life was far more intricate—and far more vast—than the quiet cul-de-sac on which it began.
And Bowie, for all its suburban ordinariness, had given me a gift: a launchpad into wonder, challenge, loss, discovery, and everything else that would come.
Chapter 3: 4004 Winchester Lane
  

A House of Contrasts
In the heart of Whitehall’s tree-lined serenity, 4004 Winchester Lane was more than just an address—it was a crucible. A place where love and tension danced together in the same rooms, where silence echoed louder than words, and where the walls bore witness to the beauty and burden of growing up in a household shaped by ambition, uncertainty, and love stretched across generational fault lines.
Brotherhood and Battle Scars
As the youngest sibling, I experienced our home as both refuge and battlefield. On good days, the air was filled with the soundtrack of my father’s cheers for the Redskins. On others, the quiet stillness carried the weight of my mother’s wanderings. She was a child bride—married at 19, pregnant soon after—and the expectations placed on her didn’t match the cultural messaging she absorbed from the world around her. She had a restless spirit, a kind of wanderlust, and at times, that meant disappearing for hours or even days at a time, something my brother recounted in more detail years later.
My brother, three years older, was my closest companion and my primary adversary. He was a mirror, a rival, a protector, and a challenger. One memory stands out—blurry in detail but sharp in consequence. We were playing with a set of colored toy blocks, and in a moment of frustration or mischief, I hurled one at his head. It struck with force, drawing blood and leaving a scar that, as far as I know, he still carries. Sorry, bro—genuinely. Like most siblings, we’ve forgiven each other a thousand times since.
The Unspoken Departures
We never really talked about our mother’s absences when we were kids. We just adapted. It wasn’t something you could easily explain to a child, and it wasn’t something we questioned out loud. My mother’s departure from our household when I was just three was a pivotal moment. The story I later learned was that she’d had an affair with a man named Mike Kinzie—who eventually left her and began a relationship with Sue Bowen, a member of Berwyn Presbyterian Church. Sue had been one of my daycare providers for a short while and eventually left her husband and son (who was my brother’s age) for Kinzie. Somehow, they stayed within the church community.
The Ties That Endured
Berwyn Presbyterian Church was a central hub in our lives for many years. It remained a point of connection even after my parents divorced. The church’s annual retreats at Chesapeake Center Camps in Port Deposit, Maryland were highlights of my childhood. I attended every summer from the time I was between second and third grade until I was about 14. It was a magical time. One retreat stands out vividly: a coed room where we all gave each other back rubs—a delightfully innocent memory that still brings a smile. Later, one of my counselors shared a phrase with me that I’ve carried ever since: “A bed is a sanctuary of sleep.” It felt deeply meditative and spiritual at the time—and still does.
A Front-Row Seat to Innovation
My father, meanwhile, was in the early stages of a remarkable career. He had just earned his Master’s in Engineering from George Washington University, following his bachelor’s from the University of Maryland. He was hired by NASA at Goddard Space Flight Center, and this alone shaped so much of what came after. Because of his work and connections, I had rare behind-the-scenes access to Goddard, Kennedy Space Center, a center in Akron, and others. I remember seeing the massive crawlers at Kennedy up close, a memory that remains crystalline in my mind. He even brought home exotic alloy satellite components—engineering failures that looked like alien artifacts. I wish I still had one.
Though my parents ultimately separated, they maintained many friendships with fellow Berwyn members. My childhood was filled with the intellectual buzz of my father’s colleagues—scientists, engineers, and artists who made our dinner parties feel like think tanks. This network, these moments, these places—they granted me early access to wonder.
The Count Chocula Goodbye
And then there was the day everything changed. I was three, my brother five. The memory is etched into my mind not with emotion, but with strange clarity. I remember standing in the kitchen, a box of Count Chocula cereal on the counter. Through the window, a U-Haul van sat in the driveway. My mother was packing. I, blissfully unaware, was riding my tricycle up and down the van’s ramp, reveling in its novelty. The contrast—the everyday cereal, the truck, the thrill of pedaling—marked a quiet fracture that would shape the chapters to come.
This wasn’t just the end of a chapter; it was a shift in the entire story. But even then, there was love—however strained, however scattered. And in the memory of that home, through the lens of time, I can still see the beauty in the storm.
Chapter 4: Wheaton and the McMansion
  

After leaving our family home, my mother moved into a simple one-room apartment in Wheaton, MD. She tried to instill a sense of wonder for my brother and me, transforming her walk-in closet into what she thought resembled the interior of a space capsule—using aluminum foil and a black light—as a tribute to our shared interest in space, an interest ignited by our father’s work with NASA. However, the rustling of the foil, which responded to every movement, produced an eerie ambiance that I found disconcerting. At the time, I lacked the words or the courage to voice my unease. To handle the behavior issues my brother and I exhibited, we took turns spending weekends with her, which often left me missing him, despite my deep admiration for him.
The Translator and the Chesapeake
Before my mother’s life intertwined with my stepfather's, there was Chuck Wieldon, a Spanish translator with a penchant for the nautical life, owning a 26-foot cabin cruiser on the Chesapeake Bay. His son Charley, a bit older than my brother and me, had been given his own 14-foot boat and exhibited the brashness often associated with indulged children. Our weekends aboard Chuck’s vessel offered us a respite on the Chesapeake Bay, a place I had come to cherish through time spent with my snowbird grandparents. However, Charley’s behavior frequently cast a shadow over these getaways.
The abrupt end to my mother's relationship with Chuck was precipitated by a particularly thoughtless incident. After a weekend excursion, we stopped at a pizza restaurant where the waitress was a native Spanish speaker. Seizing the opportunity, Chuck engaged her in an extended Spanish conversation, perhaps to showcase his linguistic proficiency. It went on for about 20 minutes, leaving my mother, brother, and me as bystanders to his display. Unbeknownst to Chuck, his behavior was deeply discourteous to my mother. This marked the end of our sailing weekends on the Chesapeake.
Enter Nolan Smith
Following the debacle with Chuck the Translator, Nolan Smith, a recent military veteran, became a part of our lives. He was studying mechanical engineering at the University of Maryland when he met my mother at Montrose Baptist Church in Wheaton, MD. The church later gained recognition in the '90s as a breeding ground for nationally recognized basketball talent. It was also the place where my brother, in the 5th grade, made a lifelong commitment to Christianity. Nolan, with his expertise in NBC warfare honed by multiple tours as a US Army veteran, brought a stringent, disciplined approach to our home life. This was in stark contrast to the more laid-back environment of my father's home. After Nolan and my mother wed in 1979, the visits to their house, now ruled by Nolan's strict regimen, were something I approached with trepidation.
Dad’s New Life and Summer Snow Forts
At the same time, my father was adapting to life as a single parent and exploring the dating scene through Parents Without Partners. He became socially active, frequently meeting women who often had daughters near my age. These interactions were usually enjoyable and informative. A particularly meaningful connection was with Barbara Fennel, a woman blessed with three captivating older daughters. During a significant snowstorm in Washington D.C., my mother, observing how much fun my brother and I were having with the other neighborhood children, helped us construct an elaborate snow fort, complete with tunnels and chambers, in a neighbor's yard, creating a wondrous reprieve from the harsh cold.
Whitehall Pool Days
During this time, and throughout my stays at my father’s home on Winchester Lane, I fondly remember summers at Whitehall Pool and Tennis Club. The pool was the hub of the community, and we all eagerly awaited its opening each year. It was a place of cherished childhood memories: the whistle signaling rest periods, the explorations of the machinery room with its complex pumps and valves, the scent of chlorine, and the repeated sunburns that now echo my father’s battle with carcinoma. Memories of the deep end, the diving boards, and the thrill of my first high dive linger.
  

Sledding with Dad

My father's lack of self-consciousness is particularly memorable. He tackled his weight gain with earnest efforts, often cycling for exercise and playing volleyball at the club, which he considered an effective workout. Watching his volleyball matches, I recall how he would confidently play shirtless, his large frame moving without restraint—something that secretly embarrassed me at the time. In hindsight, though, I understand that he had no need to consider its impact on me, nor should he have.
Life at NASA
My father was a significant figure professionally. As an Integration Manager at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, he played a crucial role in coordinating contractors for satellite components. He was essentially the final authority on what made it onto the satellite, overseeing its testing, packaging, and shipment to the launch facility. His role was critical in launch control as well.
He once confided in me about his recurring nightmare involving a catastrophic test failure with the Landsat D or Solar Maximum Mission in Building 7's vacuum chamber, ending with the satellite crashing from its test harness and being destroyed. It's not your typical nightmare. My pride in him was immense, and I couldn't help but feel a touch of smug satisfaction when he conducted tours for my Cub Scout and Boy Scout groups at Goddard Space Flight Center.
I had the privilege of frequently going to work with my father at Goddard and NASA Headquarters. As my father was a single parent, I had numerous opportunities to walk the halls of Goddard and NASA headquarters kind of like the team mascot. I got to know a bunch of my dad's colleagues who inspired my love of STEM. Goddard was particularly enthralling with its diverse buildings and the bustle of activities. There, I had free rein and often interacted with professionals working on the most advanced technology of the time. When I had questions about physics, my favorite subject, he would modestly refer me to his friend, a Nobel laureate physicist, for more comprehensive answers, as he considered himself "just an engineer."
When I asked my father questions on my favorite topic: physics, he would often defer to his friend, a cosmologist friend named Harvey for more in-depth answers. Harvey’s influence and standing in the realms of physics were clear and impactful. Harvey was a true renaissance man, seamlessly integrating his scientific acumen with a robust enthusiasm for the performing arts. His involvement in a community theater production of 'Fiddler on the Roof' stands out in my memory, showcasing his versatility and creative flair. This duality of scientific brilliance and artistic passion left an indelible mark on me, highlighting the importance and influence of cultivating diverse interests. Harvey's distinction in both the scientific and artistic communities enriched my early years, providing a model of the well-rounded intellect and cultural contributor I aspired to become.
Cocoa Beach and Kennedy Space Center
My father kept an unabridged Oxford Dictionary on his work desk, a tome that followed him from NASA Goddard to Headquarters, located near the Air & Space Museum in the heart of Washington, D.C. In times predating the allure of digital games and the internet, he would methodically peruse the dictionary during quieter moments. He marked each journey through its pages with a unique glyph, evidencing the numerous times he had traversed its contents. This meticulous practice of his was surely the wellspring of both my own and my daughter’s proficient command of the English language.
In addition to my adventures with my father at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, when I was in the second grade at Capeview Elementary, I was wrapped up in all things NASA. I knew even then that Kennedy Space Center and Cocoa Beach were the Mecca of spaceflight. I had a pretty good idea of what a great opportunity it was, even if for just a few brief months, I would get to live on the Space Coast. Evenings were spent walking the beach, searching for the best vantage point to watch nighttime launches of the Atlas launch vehicle. There were enough launches during this time that it seemed normal.
  

Ron and Ken circa 1978
While in Cocoa Beach, my father treated the whole family to a personalized, insiders-only tour of KSC. He had encyclopedic knowledge of the entire facility. Together, we toured the bunk houses for various launches, and he would point to the seat he manned for the launch. He brought us through the vehicle integration room, or at least to look through the glass at it, as they integrated the Solar Maximum Mission onto the launch vehicle. We got up close and personal with a giant crawler developed to move around the massive Saturn V, which awe inspiring at the time and to this day. Overall, this provided much of the structural framework for my lifelong advocacy of space science and science fiction.
The Townsend Influence
Another force in the development of my passion for science, in general, and aerospace science, in particular, was my father's good friend, Colonel Gene Townsend of the United States Air Force. Gene had a son around Ronald's age named Jet, with whom we shared frequent childhood adventures. But there was always this mystical element to Gene, who was actually a journalist for the Stars and Stripes magazine. However, he was an Air Force officer and I always associated a sense of wonder with him because of this, for me like having the Bionic Man as a family friend.

Neighborhood Friends and Suburban Rituals
In addition, I was lucky to have a rich circle of neighborhood friends. Together, we explored the drainage system near the pool and spent countless afternoons biking and sledding. Snowball fights and confrontations with bullies were part of the fabric of those days.
Throughout this time, my father was meeting various women via Parents Without Partners, which often led to us kids navigating the sometimes awkward, sometimes enlightening waters of adolescence together. Eventually, he formed a meaningful relationship with Barbara Fennel, a woman whose social and economic status mirrored our own, and who had two teenage daughters of her own.
Barbara, despite the ways she mirrored my mother, was not without her own imperfections. To me, she was the spitting image of Lynda Carter, who at that time graced television screens as Wonder Woman. They had been in a relationship for around a year when, without warning, Barbara withdrew from both their plan to purchase a house together and from the relationship itself—a plan that had been viable only with their combined resources.
A Violent Chapter
Barbara's abrupt exit left my father to grapple with the aftermath, both emotionally and financially. Sued by the home's owner who happened to be an attorney, my father was forced to purchase the monstrosity, stretching his finances to their limit. It was amidst this tumultuous time that Linda Franklin came into our lives through Parents Without Partners. Soon after, she moved into the house, which only served to compound the negative memories associated with it.
My most harrowing memory from that era involved an incident that began innocently enough as a child’s curiosity. I had taken apart my bedroom door lock, fascinated by its shiny, mechanical intricacies, and needed help putting it back together. When I approached Linda for help, her reaction was both unexpected and frightening. Frustrated by the disassembled lock, Linda lost her temper in a severe way. Without warning, she shoved me towards my bed with a force that was startling, and her anger didn’t subside until she had exhausted herself, landing heavy closed fist blows on my face and body, leaving her lying breathless laying on top of me on my bed, breathing hard in close proximity to my ear— not in the gentle manner of affection, but in the fraught aftermath of a severe ass whopping. Though I escaped without physical scars, the emotional impact was profound and long-lasting, leaving an indelible mark on my psyche.
The gravity of the violence was stark, leaving me to navigate through a maze of fear and pain that a child should never have to endure. Such events highlight the disparity in how different forms of abuse are recognized and addressed. The emotional scars they leave are profound and enduring, and the complexity of these experiences is not easily reconciled with the limitations of legal definitions and interventions.
Chapter 5: A Case of Projection
Diagnosed and Diminished
In the midst of this, Linda's erratic behavior led to my early introduction to mental health care. My intuition suggests that Linda learned of a new "wonder drug" for kids, Dexedrine, believed to silence them and transform even the most challenging children into model citizens. As a result, I was conveniently diagnosed with ADD and placed on the maximum dosage allowable of Dexedrine. This medication didn't merely treat symptoms; it effectively muzzled me, transforming a naturally lively child into one of the most reserved in any classroom. These measures, likely seen as a quick fix for deeper challenges, added an additional layer of complexity to my already intricate emotional landscape during those formative years.
A Broader Context Emerges
As time has passed, I’ve begun to view this part of my history within a broader context. The expansive diagnostic criteria of ADD and its frequent treatment with medication have sparked considerable debate. This trend has become so widespread that it has even led to shortages of medications like the one I was prescribed, highlighting societal implications and igniting discussions about the appropriateness of such medical interventions. Reflecting on this aspect of my past, I realize it invites a critical examination of the intersections between mental health care, individual behavior management, and the ethics of the pharmaceutical industry. My recollections of my early visits to child psychiatrists are vague; I mostly remember odd waiting rooms and practitioners with an obtuse approach to child psychology. However, they did at least listen to me, which was more than I could say for many adults in my life at the time.
Nolan’s Dual Legacy
Realizing the influence my stepfather Nolan had on my life, thanks in part to insights gained from Tony Robbins' I'm Not Your Guru, I've come to appreciate a profound truth: Nolan was instrumental in forging what I now consider my superpower. His impact, though fraught with challenges, contributed to my ability to withstand and thrive amid life's adversities.
Nolan, who became a part of our family when he married my mother in 1979, was a man of contradictions. He professed conservative Christian values publicly, yet privately, he grappled with actions that belied those avowed principles. His disciplinary methods were strict and often immediate, escalating in severity for even minor infractions. The discipline, albeit progressive in its application, was by any measure intense and unequivocally corporal. Minor transgressions could trigger a disproportionate physical response, with punishments that seemed more retributive than rehabilitative.
From Crucible to Strength
However, with the passage of time and through introspection, I have moved towards a place of forgiveness and comprehension. The resilience I possess today, the ability to navigate life's unpredictable and challenging moments, has its roots in those early, formative encounters with Nolan. They served as a rigorous crucible, unwittingly forging a resilience within me that has stood the test of time.
Chapter 6: A Christian Patchwork
Christianity, in its varied expressions, was a constant in my early life, with my biological parents originally crossing paths at Berwyn Presbyterian Church and my mother later encountering Nolan at Montrose Baptist Church. The distinct ethos of each congregation contributed to a profound sense of confusion during my formative years, ultimately becoming one of the many factors that led to my embrace of atheistic beliefs at the age of fourteen.
My upbringing was a mosaic of religious experiences: from Presbyterian to Baptist traditions, from church camps to Bible studies and mandatory chapel four days a week at a Methodist military school, and my involvement in a Lutheran Boy Scout troop. My mother’s fervent commitment to her born-again Christian faith was intense, and my brother later charted his own course within a Pentecostal church, diverging from Nolan’s and my mother’s convictions. This tapestry of spiritual environments played a significant role in shaping my early understanding of faith, its influence on personal character, and the fabric of community life.
________________


Emotional Landscapes and Silent Car Rides
My biological parents were never ones to hide their feelings, their emotions as vivid and discernible as the brightest colors. Both grappled with anger, yet it was their expressions of it that differed from my step-parents. Linda’s anger turned into something darker, a quiet cruelty that emerged as physical and emotional harm. To give Nolan the benefit of the doubt—he was from the school of hard knocks, and while I didn’t agree with it then, I am grateful for it now.
My father’s disdain for music is a prime example of his emotional transparency. Any attempt to play a tune in the car would transform his demeanor—his patience giving way to visible frustration—until he’d eventually switch off the radio. Silence was golden when it came to rides with pops.
________________


Christian Life Center: Legalism and Theater
The Christian Life Center stood as a symbol of transformation during the tumultuous period after I was moved to my mother’s house in sixth grade. Amidst the household’s religious schism—spurred by my mother and Nolan’s departure from the “too liberal” Montrose Baptist Church for the conservative Halpine Baptist Church—my brother discovered the Christian Life Center.
Merely a stone’s throw from where my father and Linda would later settle, the irony wasn’t lost on me, especially since it was Pastor Libby from this institution who would later break the grim news of my mother’s suicide. My repeated pleas to join my brother at the Christian Life Center were eventually granted. The church was steeped in legalism, promoting austere dress codes for women and a no-makeup policy that was as rigid as it was archaic.
I recall the whispers of Pastor Libby’s daughter flouting these strictures and the church’s gossip that swirled like eddies in a stagnant pond. Pastor Libby, an anointed preacher, who proclaimed the Bible as his sole authority—a claim that was as bold as it was questionable. The church’s “mission trips” to the Caribbean, masked as evangelical endeavors, seemed nothing more than tropical escapades. I sat through their post-trip presentations, skeptical of the “spiritual work” touted amid images of sandy beaches and luxury.
________________


Theatrics and Tongues
Sunday evenings and Wednesday services were theatrical displays of manipulation, where random congregants would erupt into lengthy, unintelligible tirades, purportedly speaking in tongues. The subsequent “translations” shouted by other congregants, claiming to be the words of Christ, struck me as deceitful performances—yet they were devoured by the congregation with alarming gullibility.
Despite my attempts to immerse myself, to lift my hands in worship and echo the choruses, I felt a growing dissonance. I was proselytizing a faith that intoxicated me with belonging, while my rational mind—nurtured by the writings of Asimov and Heinlein—recognized the absurdity of it all.
________________


Disillusionment and Departure
By fourteen, the dissonance crescendoed to a rejection of Christianity. The denominational rivalries, the disparaging remarks about other faiths—Unitarian Universalists dubbed as devilish, atheists as worse—cemented my disbelief. How could I subscribe to a belief system fragmented by its own adherents’ discord?
Years later, as a single parent, I sought a broader perspective for my daughter, leading us to the Unitarian Universalist Church in Tampa. There, amidst a diversity of thought and a religious education program unconcerned with dogma, she found her fondest memories.
This chapter in my life illustrated the paradox of faith—a concept meant to unify yet so often divides—setting the stage for the philosophical introspections that would come during my time of incarceration, a narrative for another time.
Chapter 7: It’s either me or him, round one.
By the end of 1979, my father’s assignment to the Solar Maximum Mission satellite took him temporarily to Kennedy Space Center. We relocated to a picturesque condo on Cocoa Beach, Florida—a world away from the dreary, cold winters of Maryland. The warmth of the sun, the crash of waves, and the salt in the air brought with them the illusion of a fresh start.
I enrolled in the second grade at Cape View Elementary, a school so close to the beach it felt more like a dream than a place of routine. My brother Ronald was placed in the fifth grade.

A Clash Between Brothers
Despite the tropical setting and the opportunity for renewal, tension brewed in our tight quarters. My brother and I, both reeling in our own ways from the turbulence of our young lives, often clashed. One night, only two weeks into our new life in Florida, an argument erupted. Like most sibling arguments, it was trivial, fleeting… but it became a turning point.
Linda, either unwilling or incapable of navigating the natural friction between two growing boys, presented my father with an ultimatum: “It’s either him or me.” More precisely, she painted Ronald as the source of all discord. She would later do the same to me.
When someone uses children as a scapegoat for their own failings, their pattern eventually shifts from one target to the next. Ronald was first. I would be next. More on that later.
________________


The Decision
In a decision that would reverberate for the rest of our lives, my father sent Ronald back to Maryland, back to our mother and Nolan, who had since married her. The decision wasn’t just difficult—it was devastating. It sent a message, intentional or not, that love could be conditional. That the less difficult child—read: the less resistant—would remain, while the more vocal or independent could be cast off.
________________


Fallout
That moment fractured something between Ronald and me, though we wouldn’t fully grasp it at the time. I imagine it left him feeling abandoned. For me, it was confusing—proof that the ground beneath my feet wasn’t as solid as I’d hoped. My brother and I were on separate paths now, paths that wouldn’t converge again for some time.
And Linda? Her pattern of projection and control had just begun. That night wasn’t just a family argument—it was the first real battle in a longer war over identity, belonging, and survival.
Chapter 8: The Padded Room
A Disclaimer and a Lens
Let me preface this with a clear statement: I am not a licensed mental health professional. But during my adult years—especially during my incarceration—I immersed myself in the study of psychology. I read a book every three days on average, devouring literature on trauma, psychiatry, neurobiology, and healing. Still, it’s not my later understanding that defines what follows—it’s the raw experience of living through it.
This chapter isn’t about diagnostics; it’s about memory. It’s about what it felt like to be a child on the receiving end of misplaced resentment and institutional labels, to live under the pressure of adult dysfunctions that had nothing to do with me—and everything to do with them.
The Weight of Resentment
From the beginning, Linda’s aggression toward me wasn’t subtle. It was visceral. Her bitterness seeped into our daily lives, finding release in emotional hostility. Whether it was due to her own traumas, her failed marriage, or a deep-seated disdain for the men in her life, my brother and I became targets. My father—brilliant in his own right, but deeply conflict-averse—was her willing participant. Perhaps she chose him precisely for that reason.
My father once told me that the only time my mother ever truly respected him was when he gave her an ultimatum. Ironically, that moment fractured our family. She left—but her departure wasn’t just the result of a broken marriage. It was the result of a slow unraveling, sparked by expectations she couldn’t live up to and compounded by secrets that only emerged years later.
Church Affairs and Collateral Damage
It wasn’t until I got out of prison—riding in a rented Chevy Malibu with my brother, my first car ride in 8.5 years—that I learned the whole truth. My brother shared that our mother had been having an affair with a man named Tim Kinsey, a member of our church community. Eventually, Kinsey married Sue Bowen, who had once been my daycare provider and was married at the time to a man very much like my father—quiet, hard-working, reliable.
These entanglements didn’t just break hearts—they broke families. Our church, Berwyn Presbyterian, which had once been a spiritual and social anchor for both families, quietly fractured under the weight of these betrayals. Kinsey didn't just steal my mother—he helped rewrite the destinies of two households.
The Germanic Mold
Linda, for her part, brought her own baggage. Raised in a strict Germanic household in Cleveland, Ohio, her mother and grandmother instilled a brand of discipline that blurred into cruelty. Her first marriage had ended in infidelity—fueling a distrust that would later be weaponized against the children in her second household. My father became her new project—malleable, earnest, and easy to reshape into the image of a “provider,” even if that meant sacrificing his sons to maintain domestic peace.
Drugging the Child, Silencing the Noise
The hostility she showed toward my brother quickly shifted to me once he was gone. When I was still in kindergarten, Linda took me to a child psychiatrist. Her goal was not healing—it was silence. The diagnosis came quickly: ADD, with a prescription for Dexedrine, the highest dosage legally prescribed at the time.
Dexedrine did exactly what she wanted: it shut me down. I became quiet, obedient, and thin—qualities that finally won me some degree of social approval. But without the medication, my weight ballooned, and with it, my self-esteem collapsed. Those early years taught me the power of conformity. If you were small and quiet, the world—at least Linda—left you alone.
Inside the Padded Room
At the end of second grade, my world turned upside down again. I was admitted to the four-orange unit of Washington Children's Hospital, a ward specializing in child psychiatry. It was run like a behavioral boot camp. A tiered reward system encouraged absolute compliance, and I—ever the perfectionist—rose to the top level quickly. But Linda insisted I remain.
The ward was filled with broken children. One had brain damage from ingesting rat poison. Another was an anorexic Asian boy who painted beautifully and barely spoke. On one occasion, Chinese hot mustard was served at a birthday celebration—one of those negligent oversights that led to panic, tears, and emergency care.
Despite all that, I was kept there for 11 weeks. In the end, I was released with a reaffirmed ADD diagnosis. Early suspicions of bipolar disorder were dismissed. I was sent home with a prescription—this time, a moderate dose of Dexedrine that wasn’t enough to curb my appetite, but just enough to keep me sedated.
Metro Lessons and Unintended Gifts
One unexpected blessing emerged from that chapter: freedom. My weekly visits to the psychiatrist meant solo trips on the Washington Metro from our home in Wheaton to Silver Spring. No smartphones. No distractions. Just a boy navigating a city on his own. These rides were moments of clarity. They taught me independence, self-reliance, and street-level observation long before I had words for such things.
Hints of Remorse
Linda never apologized to me directly. But years later, my brother told me she had expressed regret—acknowledging that she had unfairly projected her rage and unresolved trauma onto us. Maybe it was sincere. Maybe it was just a way to ease her own conscience. But I’ve chosen to accept it.
Healing Without Pills
By the time I entered high school, I was done with Dexedrine. Instead, I found natural regulators: football, caloric restraint, and structure. Slowly, I clawed back the voice I had lost. I shed the imposed silence. I found my stride.
These trials, painful as they were, forged the resilience I carry to this day. The padded room didn’t break me. It built me.
Chapter 9: Reverse Cinderella Story
A New Family, An Uneven Scale
The reshaping of our family through remarriage didn’t simply add new faces—it redrew the boundaries of attention, affection, and fairness. Katherine Franklin, my step-sister, entered my life as a fixture in the new household formed by my father and Linda. Katherine had her own challenges—chief among them, an absent biological father who was busy sowing children across multiple households. But in our home, my father became her stand-in father, and his devotion to her stood in stark contrast to the uneven support my brother and I received.
Linda’s maternal energies flowed freely toward her daughter, while my brother and I often felt like second-class citizens in our own home. Katherine had privileges we didn’t. One of the earliest symbols of that disparity was after-school care. Katherine got to go home. I was sent to daycare. She had freedom. I had supervision and the subtle shame of not being trusted with the same.
Freedom Comes With a Catch
By the time I reached sixth grade, I was finally allowed to come home after school like Katherine. It was a turning point—one that I had long looked forward to. But with this new autonomy came increased exposure to the imbalanced dynamics of our blended family. Without the buffer of external supervision, conflict became more common.
It didn’t take long for things to boil over.
The Day the Air Turned Hot
One afternoon, a small argument between me and Katherine—typical sibling squabbling—got out of hand. She said something snide. I said something back. Whatever it was, it was nothing new. But in this household, Katherine always had home-field advantage.
She took her grievance to Linda, who—predictably—took her side without question. I felt cornered, powerless, and enraged by the injustice of it all. So I acted out.
My act of rebellion? Turning the heat up in her room—a childish, petty revenge, sure, but it felt like the only control I had in that moment.
The Punishment Didn’t Fit the Crime
You’d think I had set the house on fire. Linda’s reaction was swift, severe, and, as far as I was concerned, grossly disproportionate. What might’ve been a teachable moment in another family became a power play. Linda issued an ultimatum to my father.
And just like that, for the second time in my life, my father chose peace with his wife over standing by his son.
I was removed from the home.
Ejected, Again
I wasn’t just grounded. I was ejected—cast out like a piece that no longer fit the puzzle. The pain of that rejection was sharp and enduring. It wasn’t the first time I’d been chosen against, and it wouldn’t be the last. But this one cut especially deep, because there was no pretense of fairness anymore.
It wasn’t about right or wrong. It was about who belonged, and who didn’t.
The Echoes of Displacement
That experience—trivial as it might seem on the surface—was anything but. It wasn’t just a heated room or a sibling spat. It was a moment that exposed the core truth of our household: some of us were permanent residents, and others were barely tolerated guests.
I wasn’t just punished. I was discarded.
That singular decision would go on to shape my sense of worth, of justice, and of what it means to be chosen—or left behind.
Chapter 10: Out of the fire, into the Frying Pan
A Jarring Transition
The fallout from being ejected from my father's home marked the beginning of a particularly turbulent chapter. I was pulled from familiar halls, separated from lifelong friends, and thrust into an entirely new middle school in a different county—where I quickly became the target of bullying and isolation. Gone were the routines and small comforts of familiarity; in their place was a cold start that tested every inch of my emotional resilience.
Welcome Home... Now Empty Your Pockets
When I arrived at my mother and stepfather Nolan’s home, I was met not with warmth, but scrutiny. Every possession I brought with me—every book, trinket, and item of clothing—was thoroughly inspected for "contraband." It was a humiliating experience, one that left a lasting impression. It wasn’t just my privacy that was violated—it was the message that I wasn’t fully trusted or truly welcome. It was clear that I was not entering a home, but a regime.
The Regimen Begins
Nolan, fresh from military service and suddenly father to two stepchildren, responded to the chaos with what he knew best: structure, discipline, and conditioning. In all fairness, he was trying—Ron and I became his mission. And while some of the tactics may have been questionable, I can now see his intent: to forge something strong from something broken.
I was put on a strict schedule. Trumpet practice exceeded school requirements, and I was drilled on physical fitness: daily push-ups, sit-ups, and calisthenics. Every infraction resulted in lines—pages of precise, behavior-specific sentences like:
“I will pick up all of the trash and dog doo immediately upon arrival home or face the consequences.”
Over time, this discipline became its own kind of meditation. A hundred afternoons filled with waxing, wiping, writing, lifting, pushing, breathing... wax on, wax off.
Household Labor and the Manual of Expectations
Nolan even handed me a cleaning manual. I learned to clean the toilet, polish a sink, scrub the floors—all to military spec. One recurring task involved cleaning the utility bathroom sink that Nolan used after working on the car. He’d leave it coated in degreaser and grime, and I was expected to return it to a mirror shine. His philosophy was simple:
“I have to work on the car. This is the least you can do.”
Dark Rooms and Five-Minute Penalties
Discipline extended beyond the chores. I had been diagnosed with ADD, and in church, I was... well, fidgety. Nolan’s solution? For every fidget, five more minutes.
Back at home, I would be made to sit in a dark bathroom, one minute for every infraction. No sound. No light. Just the hum of the fan and the random inspections. If I failed the test? Corporal punishment followed.
Many of those minutes in the dark stacked into hours. The bruises faded, but the memory of being punished not for disobedience, but for simply being myself—a child with neurodivergent wiring—has never truly left me.
The Shot Heard 'Round the Campsite
One incident at Little Bennett State Park cemented how tenuous my safety really was. We often camped there in our travel trailer, and it should have been a place of peace. But on one hike, as my brother and I made our way through the woods—completely unaware—a .357-caliber pellet struck me directly in the forehead.
Nolan, sitting with my mother over 75 yards away, had fired it intentionally.
The sharp sting. The swelling bruise. The stunned silence. The shock wasn’t just physical. It was the realization that pain could arrive silently, at the hands of someone who believed it was “for our own good.”
Our Dog Rover: A Bright Spot
Not all memories from this time were bleak. Rover, our loyal Chesapeake Bay Retriever, was a constant, comforting presence. He weighed in at about 75 pounds and carried himself with the quiet nobility I now see echoed in my current companion, Daisy, a British Staffordshire. Though of different breeds, they share the same soulful gravity, and I like to think their consciousness resides on the same wavelength.
Of course, character-building came with responsibilities—namely, Rover’s copious daily dookie. Like clockwork, I was its sole custodian.
My Paper Route, My Ritual
As my brother entered high school and began preparing for his next chapter, I inherited his paper route. For nearly two years, I delivered the Gaithersburg Gazette up and down Shady Grove Road.
Every (other) Wednesday, a bundle of papers would arrive at the doorstep. After school, I’d stuff them, bag them, and walk them from house to house. In those quiet walks, I learned rhythm, responsibility, and what it meant to keep showing up—even when no one was watching.
Looking Ahead
The tension and discipline of this era left deep grooves in my character. They taught me how to operate under pressure, how to compartmentalize pain, and how to extract meaning from menial tasks. But they also raised questions that I would only begin to answer in adulthood—about authority, fairness, and the blurred line between discipline and abuse.
And so, from the darkness of the bathroom to the early-morning light of paper routes, I began forging something of my own.
A self. A resilience. A spark.
Chapter 11: My JOY from Skating
The Calm Before the Fall
The incident with the .357 pellet wasn’t just a one-off moment of violence—it was a signal flare, a precursor to deeper turbulence brewing beneath the surface. That same eighth-grade year, I found myself tethered between escalating tension at home and the bright flickers of joy that still managed to break through, especially in the company of a new best friend: Dino Martinez.
We met at Great Skate in Rockville, MD, a mecca for adolescent energy and motion, where friendships were born not over words, but shared laps around the rink and synchronized side-glides to Top 40 beats. Dino and I quickly became inseparable. Our friendship was sealed not just at the skate rink, but also in those formative moments spent at the local ballpark or on one particularly unforgettable day at Kings Dominion, the legendary amusement park.
That trip—filled with laughter, adrenaline, and the fizzy excitement of youth—took place just weeks before the end of the school year. And while it should’ve been an unqualified high point, I carried with me a strange sense of foreboding, a gut-level feeling that the upcoming class field trip to the same park would not end so well.
That story, and what followed, is for a later chapter. But the shadow it cast was real.
Where the Wheels Healed Me
Amid the looming uncertainty of home life, I found an unexpected source of transcendence—skating. Inline, quad, ice, or rink—any surface, any style. There was no place in my young life where I felt more free, more myself, or more in control than when I was in motion.
Skating wasn’t just exercise. It was escape.
It was therapy.
It was flight.
The moment my wheels hit the floor or my blades sliced the ice, the noise of the world faded away. The punishments. The isolation. The rules. All of it became background static.
And like all good stories of adolescence, mine includes a first kiss—a fleeting, electrified moment with Lisa Denison. It happened at the rink, naturally, amidst the pulse of music and the shimmer of disco lights. Just a peck, but it meant everything. For a few minutes, I wasn’t a kid dodging tension—I was a kid feeling normal.
More Than a Hobby—A Lifeline
As I grew, skating became more than a passion—it became a core part of my identity. Even through life’s hurricanes, it never left me. Not when I moved. Not when I faltered. Not even when my home was looted in 2009.
That moment—devastating on many levels—nearly robbed me of everything I had accumulated. But one item survived: my premium Rollerblade skates with 100mm wheels. They weren’t just rescued by chance. My brother went out of his way to retrieve them.
That act meant the world.
He wasn’t just saving a pair of skates. He was saving a piece of me—a thread of light that had carried me through some of my darkest nights.
Skating was, and still is, my joy.
It taught me rhythm, balance, and grace. It gave me confidence, connection, and clarity. And long before I knew anything about resilience, it taught me how to glide through pain without letting it stick.
Chapter 12: Dino’s Hot Older Sister
Brotherhood on Wheels
As I look back on my adolescence, few names bring a smile to my face like Dino Martinez. He wasn’t just my best friend—he was the first guy I bonded with in that totally unspoken, guy-code kind of way. Great Skate in Rockville was our sanctuary. Every weekend, we rolled through the rink, dodging fluorescent lights and teenage heartbreak, immersed in the freedom that only came from speed, music, and low lighting.
It was more than skating—it was social survival. Dino and I were figuring it all out together: how to be cool, how to talk to girls, how to stand out without standing out too much.
And Then… There Was Her
Now, I know what you’re thinking: "Was I just using Dino to get close to his older sister?" Let me clear that up real quick:
Negatory, Ghost Rider. The pattern is full.
Dino was my guy. We had our own vibe. But like every true adolescent friendship between two dudes, there was always some kind of storyline involving girls. This one just happened to involve his older sister—and oh man, did she make an impression.
The Kings Dominion Trip
It was the end of eighth grade when Dino invited me on what I can only describe as the perfect teen adventure: a trip to Kings Dominion with his youth group. Roller coasters, group chaperones, greasy pizza, and just the right amount of awkward.
And there she was—his sister, older, confident, and impossibly cool. I don’t even remember what we talked about, but I remember the way each roller coaster ride next to her felt like its own mini-lifetime. The way the wind hit differently when she was screaming beside me. The electricity of brushing shoulders in the seat. It was euphoric.
No kiss, no late-night confession. Just one perfect day that etched itself into memory as only adolescent infatuation can.
A Moment That Lingered
That was the first and last time I saw her. Just one sun-soaked, neon-splashed day in a sea of thousands that followed. But it stuck. Not because of some fantasy or "what if," but because it reminded me how much joy could come from just being seen—just being there, soaking up the magic of the moment without needing it to lead to anything else.
Some memories are like that. They ask for nothing in return. They just are.
And that’s enough.
Chapter 13: Not King’s Dominion Again
Permission Denied
As Redland Middle School prepared for its traditional eighth-grade trip to Kings Dominion, my heart carried a mix of anticipation and dread. Just weeks earlier, I’d been to the park with Dino’s youth group—a highlight of my young life—and I knew the odds of being allowed to go again were slim. Nolan’s frugality was legendary. Even I couldn’t justify a second trip, especially one that required shelling out cash.
Still, I delayed asking—a classic procrastinator’s move—waiting until the last possible moment. Maybe, just maybe, a last-minute plea would soften him. It didn’t.
His refusal was swift and absolute, but it wasn’t just the denial that hurt—it was the finality of it. The sense that he hadn’t even considered my request on merit, but dismissed it because it was me asking.
Standing Tall
The confrontation that followed wasn’t just about a theme park. It was a symbolic eruption of all the suppressed resentment and emotion that had been simmering beneath the surface of our relationship.
I stood toe-to-toe with him—me, 6’1” and still growing; him, a full 6’4”, hardened by military discipline and years of wielding authority. But this time, I didn’t back down. The argument turned physical. I can’t remember how it started, only that I refused to be manhandled again. I pushed back.
And to my surprise—maybe even his—it ended in something of a draw. I got hit. He got hit. We both walked away different. Something shifted that day—not necessarily trust or warmth—but perhaps a sliver of grudging respect. Maybe not in words. But in how he saw me afterward.
The Lessons of Little Bennett
These moments—like the Little Bennett pellet gun incident and the Kings Dominion fight—weren’t isolated. They were points on a trajectory that forced me to confront power, authority, and my own sense of self-worth. It was the early formation of a boundary, one carved in bruises and defiance, but a boundary nonetheless.
What I learned wasn’t some dramatic moral about forgiveness or love. What I learned was how to survive. How to stand up. How to protect what little sense of self I had left.
High Adventure, Low Humanity
Later that summer, I embarked on what should have been one of the most memorable experiences of my youth: a high adventure trek with the National Capital Area Scouting Council to Philmont Scout Ranch in New Mexico.
The challenge was physical: 110 miles across rough terrain in 10 days. I was the youngest in the group, already well over six feet tall, and eager to prove myself. I signed up for the most grueling route they offered, hoping to earn respect.
What I got instead was something very different.
Philmont: A Harsh Awakening
I talked too much. I know that now. Chalk it up to ADHD, nerves, or the need to be liked, but my incessant commentary—trying too hard to be funny or fit in—didn’t go over well.
The older scouts bullied me mercilessly. At first, it was teasing, then name-calling, and eventually outright mockery. No one stood up for me. There were no adult interventions, no moments of redemption. It was ten long days of isolation, trudging up rocky slopes and pretending the words didn’t sting.
By the time we made our way home, I wasn’t just exhausted—I was disillusioned. The brotherhood Scouting promised never showed up for me out there. What should’ve been a capstone experience became another notch on my belt of rejection.
The journey began, as so many do, in chaos.
Departing from Montgomery Mall
On June 22, 1986, I arrived at Montgomery Mall just after 7 a.m., greeted by disarray. My gear was all over the place—nothing seemed squared away, and everything was either too high or packed too long. The crew, buzzing with early morning energy, aimed to depart by 8:30, but the first crew leader, Roger Knight, had forgotten his sleeping gear, delaying our launch. We didn’t roll out until 9:00 p.m., and the trip was an overnight slog: no sleep, just endless dark highway.
Bowling, Cold Showers, and Kansas
Somewhere in Illinois, we stayed at an Air Force Base where I bowled ten full games before crashing. We pushed west, spending the night at Fort Riley, Kansas, where the shower situation was grim. I opted for a cold gym shower rather than wait in line. The next morning, I was already forgetting what day it was—everything blurred in transit.
We watched a forgettable G-rated movie called My Chester—the kind of film that tries hard to be deep without having anything to say. What stuck with me more than the film was the monotony. Kansas was already wearing on me, but Philmont was the light at the end of the tunnel.
Arrival at Philmont – June 27
At last, we rolled into Philmont Scout Ranch. The staff welcomed us with a stage production that was surprisingly cool. I met a kid from Florida, near where I used to live, and made a note to find him again—he’d pop up later at Mount Baldy. The altitude already had me winded, and a twenty-minute introductory session in that thin air felt like an hour. I had my Eureka Timberline tent and my cook gear ready to go.
First Day on the Trail – June 28
We were up before 6:00. Breakfast was pancakes and cereal, surprisingly decent. I made a monster 3" sandwich for lunch that was downright amazing. My pack weighed 47 pounds—and let me tell you, I felt every ounce of it.
That day’s hike was listed as 2.5 miles as the crow flies, which turned into about eight brutal miles up and down terrain that definitely wasn’t meant for flatlanders. We set up camp, cooked dinner of mashed potatoes, pork, beans, and beef broth, and crashed.
Mt. Phillips – July 1
The next major milestone was Mount Phillips—and it was a beast. We had hot chocolate for breakfast, then did black powder rifle shooting and hatchet throwing before the climb.
The hike was steep, but I pushed myself hard. Some crews took over an hour and a half just to descend. We climbed it in the same time they took to come down. The view at the top? Unforgettable. We ate lunch at the summit, and for a few brief minutes, I felt something like peace.
From there, we made our way to Red Hill Camp. Brian, one of my crewmates, and I built a mini dam in a nearby stream. It’s funny how something so small can stay with you years later.
July 2 – Mines, Showers, and Volleyball
After conquering Mt. Phillips, the next day was relatively easy. We hiked 9 miles, mostly downhill, passing through Cypress Mine Camp where we toured Contention Mine. The rails were rusted, and the walls dripped water like a forgotten cave. We even got to try some blacksmithing before pushing on to Cameroncito.
At Cameroncito, I finally had a shower, then played volleyball—and won every game.
I can still remember scribbling in my journal that night:
"If you haven’t noticed, my journal entries have been getting shorter lately. So I thought I’d fill the rest of the page with this note to ease off.”
July 4 – 13 Miles of Pain
That Friday, we hiked 13 brutal miles. We left Vista Grande, hit the main Philmont road, and then ascended Dean Canyon. It was scorching. At Head of Dean, we stopped for lunch, where I saw the Central Florida crew, who looked like they’d been roasted in a coal-burning furnace. They were burnt, blistered, and broken—exactly how I felt.
We did Dean’s Challenge, which was more boring than it sounds, and then hiked out again. That night, I did the dishes—again—and signed off the journal with tired hands.
Reflections
Reading these entries now, I can still feel the dust in my throat, the ache in my shoulders, the burn of rejection, and the quiet joy of personal milestones: a summit reached, a sandwich savored, a stream dammed.
Philmont wasn’t just a Boy Scout trip. It was a rite of passage. A microcosm of my adolescence: exhaustion, moments of friendship, persistent bullying, and bursts of joy amidst the grind.
It wasn’t perfect, but it was real. And it helped forge something lasting in me: the ability to keep walking, no matter how heavy the pack.
The Shelter of Science
High school came, and with it, a strange salvation: academics. Amid the chaos at home, the instability of switching schools, and the constant challenge of finding belonging, I could always rely on one thing: my brain.
I wasn’t just a good student—I was obsessed with learning, especially science. Physics and chemistry were safe havens, places where things made sense. Laws were consistent, forces predictable, outcomes logical. In a world where adults lied, bullies ruled, and families fell apart, science never did me dirty.
And in those classes, between the pages of textbooks and scribbled equations, I found a version of myself that no one could push around.
Chapter 14: My lifelong love affair with Sci Fi
Tom Swift and the Birth of Wonder
In the whirlwind of my early life, marked by instability and contradiction, one force remained constant—Science Fiction. My enduring relationship with the genre began during a vulnerable moment: a hospital stay for an emergency appendectomy. In an unexpected show of kindness, my stepmother brought me several Tom Swift novels. Their vividly illustrated covers—bold depictions of futuristic cities, plasma jets, and heroic young adventurers—offered a gateway to a universe far removed from the sterile smell of antiseptic and hospital sheets. These weren’t just stories; they were refuges.
I devoured them. Page after page, I was transported into a world where curiosity, intellect, and courage solved problems—not fists, not fury. These books became the first stepping stones in what would evolve into a lifelong exploration of speculative thought, big ideas, and narrative transcendence.
The Great Introductions: Niven, Bester, and Beyond
My brother, always a few steps ahead in reading level and taste, widened the scope of my science fiction education. It was through him that I was introduced to Ringworld by Larry Niven, and The Stars My Destination by Alfred Bester. These stories weren’t mere escapism—they were thought experiments wrapped in adventure, metaphors encoded in laser fire and spaceship hulls.
Soon, I was reading Robert A. Heinlein, Isaac Asimov, James P. Hogan, Frederick Pohl, and dozens more. I began to sense a pattern—not just in the stories, but in the way they sharpened my thinking. They weren’t just entertaining. They were preparing me. For life. For skepticism. For the realization that the world was not as simple, clean, or logical as adults made it out to be.
Heinlein and the Expansion of Morality
Heinlein, in particular, left a mark. His stories were unapologetically bold in their willingness to question social norms. Time Enough for Love and The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress didn’t shy away from complex issues—whether that meant exploring polyamory, privacy rights, libertarianism, or the foundations of human dignity.
Even as a teen, I knew I was reading something that was culturally radioactive. Heinlein’s work was a litmus test for personal freedom, for thinking outside the constraints imposed by schoolteachers, pastors, or even parents. His characters often lived by the credo that the privacy and sovereignty of adults, in mutual respect and consent, were sacrosanct. In a household full of rules, expectations, and watchful eyes, this was... electrifying.
Asimov, the Atheist, and Me
While Heinlein challenged my sense of morality and personal liberty, Isaac Asimov shaped the scaffold of my early philosophical and religious outlook. The Foundation series, the Robot novels, and his many essays painted a universe governed not by supernatural intervention, but by rationality, self-correcting systems, and ethical responsibility.
For a long time, I would have told you that Asimov’s secular, humanist worldview made the most sense. I proudly identified as an atheist for most of my young adult life, embracing logic over faith, skepticism over mysticism.
The Ontological Rabbit Hole
But life, as it tends to do, complicates things. In recent years, with the United States government acknowledging the reality of UFO phenomena, my mind has reopened the gates that Asimov once closed. Something about these revelations—small as they are—tore open a question I thought was long sealed: What is reality, really?
The notion that these “aliens” might not be extraterrestrial at all, but rather human descendants—time travelers—threw everything into flux. Suddenly, the frameworks built by Asimov and the Humanists no longer felt complete. Was the soul a real thing? Could consciousness transcend linear time? Was reincarnation a metaphor... or a mechanism?
A Return to the Stars
My reading list, once filled with Niven’s orbital megastructures and Asimov’s psychohistorians, now included David Grusch, Jacques Vallée, Whitley Strieber, and Jason Reza Jorjani. I returned to James P. Hogan, particularly The Genesis Machine and Inherit the Stars, with a whole new lens.
It turns out that Sci-Fi—far from being fiction—is a language for exploring the deepest philosophical truths we can’t yet prove but feel, deeply, to be real. Through it, I’ve come to believe that we are more than flesh. That we are consciousness wearing biology. That our stories, even the imagined ones, are echoes of the greater reality we’ve forgotten.
Closing Thought
If I ever seem a little too obsessed with speculative fiction, it’s because it saved me. Not once. Countless times. When home wasn’t safe, when truth felt slippery, when faith seemed false or harsh or hypocritical, science fiction offered me the stars.
And somewhere out there—whether in a Dyson sphere, a Martian colony, or the memory palace of some post-human AI—I like to think there's still a version of me gliding between galaxies, chasing the next chapter.
Chapter 15: The Soul Field
Beyond Flesh and Time
My conviction now leans strongly toward the belief that we possess something eternal—a soul, a field, a resonance that stretches beyond this life. I don’t claim to know the specifics. The afterlife, the multiverse, reincarnation—these are complex mysteries. But I am nearly certain that some essence of us continues on.
This sense of something more isn’t rooted in religious doctrine, nor is it a byproduct of wishful thinking. It’s the convergence of science fiction, ontological inquiry, and patterns too compelling to ignore. The strange truths hinted at in government UFO disclosures. The eerie continuity in synchronicities. The odd familiarity of a dog's gaze that says, "I know you."
Holding Space for the Unseen
I imagine that somewhere, maybe in a timeline yet unwound, my mother, father, grandparents, and my daughter Natalie are still with me. Not metaphorically. Literally. In a kind of persistence of memory, encoded in whatever physics lies behind this veil.
I often think: if future humans exist—and I now believe they do—might they read these words someday? Might they see this version of me, preserved in data, DNA, or quantum pattern, and choose to pay a visit?
If so: Hello. I’m here.
Max and Peter
My dog Max, a loving, cuddly little Shih Tzu with a knowing presence, reminds me daily of someone else: Peter Linzmeyer, my old friend who helped guide me into the Navy. Something about Max’s demeanor, the soulful way he looks at me when I speak, makes me wonder—did Peter come back? Is that even the right question?
Maybe soul energy isn’t tied to a single vessel, but flows like a river, branching into forms that feel right.
Brotherhood Forged in Summer
The transition from middle to high school is never easy, but it was during this pivotal time that I forged one of the most enduring friendships of my youth—with Bradford “Chuck” Clark. Our paths crossed through school and scouting, and from the outset, there was a natural camaraderie. Over time, our bond deepened, cemented by shared experiences in Boy Scouts, band corridors, adolescent mischief, and those early glimmers of independence that define your teenage years.
That bond would eventually stretch to places like Rehoboth Beach, Delaware, where—between our junior and senior years—we reunited with David Laime, my roommate from Randolph-Macon Academy. Chuck’s family condo provided our base of operations, and I found lodging in a nearby boarding house run by my employer’s mother. She was a notorious hoarder, and living in that chaos made the freedom of the beach strip feel like pure oxygen.
Mustang Summers and Cigarette Pacts
One of the greatest perks of that summer: Chuck’s family had not one, but two Ford Mustangs—a red LX and a black GT convertible. We cruised the Rehoboth Beach strip in those cars like we owned the place. Every engine rev, every lap down the avenue felt like a declaration of independence. We were young, wild, and temporarily immortal.
But to rewind: that friendship with Chuck really took root in the summer before ninth grade. On a blazing day in Gaithersburg, full of that teenage hunger to test limits, we talked some random adult into buying us cigarettes outside a convenience store on Redland Road. We walked back with our illicit haul, victorious, only to pause mid-stride and make a different kind of pact:
We were joining the football team.
It wasn't planned. It was instinctive. A bold, reckless choice—but the kind that would define our high school years.
Lower Than Whale Shit
Day one of football camp at Magruder High School was something out of a boot camp fever dream. They herded us into a classroom, sat us down in the stifling heat, and our coach took a piece of chalk to the board. He drew an ocean. At the bottom, he sketched a pile of whale shit and proclaimed, without irony:
“You are lower than whale shit. And that is where you’re starting.”
It was an initiation. Raw, undiplomatic, and strangely galvanizing. We all got the message: from here on out, life was going to demand more from us.
From Art to Combat
I’d spent middle school involved in band and chorus, and while I hadn't pursued organized sports at Redland, I had studied Shōrin-ryū karate under Sensei Jim Starjill. I didn’t climb far—only made it to yellow belt—but the discipline, the stance work, and the mental training stayed with me.
Little did I know, those principles would later carry me through the grueling crucible of high school wrestling at Randolph-Macon Academy. But football came first.
Offensive Tackle, Offensive Mindset
At Magruder High School, I found myself stepping into a role I’d never envisioned: starting offensive tackle on the JV team. The transformation was almost cinematic. From awkward, artistic band kid to trench-footed lineman battling it out in the dirt. It wasn’t easy. Nothing about it was.
But I was finally in motion.
I had direction.
I had teammates.
Chuck was one of the real ones—a soul brother in a long line of them.
And for once, the momentum was with me.
Chapter 16: Mom’s promotion to Angel
Friday Night Lights, Then Silence
After our first regular-season JV game against Walt Whitman High School, I walked through the front door, amped from the adrenaline, expecting to see my mom—to tell her all about it. But the house was still. Too still. No note, no TV, no warm kitchen light—just silence.
Then the phone rang.
It was my dad. His voice was off, but he said he was coming to pick me up. I didn’t question it.
We drove back to his place in North Potomac without a single word. The silence in that car was heavier than anything I’d felt up to that point. And when we arrived, the weight dropped.
Pastor Libby from my brother’s Pentecostal church was there, waiting.
He broke the news: my mother had taken her own life.
Time stopped.
The Breaking Point
In the weeks leading up to her death, my mom was unraveling. She’d been urged by her job at Physicians Weight Loss Center in Gaithersburg to take two weeks off. A kidney infection had taken a toll, but so had the emotional strain of a recent trip to the West Coast with Nolan. The purpose? To scout for potential safe zones in the event of nuclear war—yes, really. That trip left her physically and emotionally depleted, and our finances, already tight, were further strained.
My mother and Nolan were both college graduates, but even with degrees, money was a constant stressor. Nolan, ever the survivalist, took frugality to extremes:
* The water heater had extra insulation and a timer.
* Powdered milk was the norm.
* Nothing, absolutely nothing, went to waste.
He did what he thought was best. I can even say I learned from it—just not in the same way. I’ve always sought to increase income, not just cut costs. But I understood the logic. Nolan’s discipline was military-grade, and the house ran like a submarine.
But even airtight systems can leak.
Final Descent
My mom, a registered nurse with deep knowledge of medicine and a lifetime of pain behind her, had long battled bipolar disorder. She knew what her medication could do. It was prescribed to help—but when wielded as a weapon, it became fatal.
After a heated argument with Nolan, she did what she always did: she left. Normally, she’d take the car and just drive until the edge of her thoughts rounded out. This time, Nolan, out of concern—maybe even fear—disabled both cars.
He thought he was protecting her.
He wasn’t.
The Final Walk
I don’t know exactly what she was thinking, but I imagine she was proud. Stubborn. Maybe ashamed. She didn’t go back to the house to ask for help. Instead, she left on foot.
Somewhere along the way, she came across a completely unrelated car accident. It was there—out on the shoulder of some Maryland road—that she finally asked for help.
An ambulance was called. They rushed her to the hospital.
But it was too late.
Cardiac arrest.
Just like that, she was gone.
The Aftermath
It was seismic. The kind of event that doesn’t just hit—it reverberates for decades. Everything about that moment—my JV jersey still damp, my cleats still dusty, the awkward silence on the ride to my dad’s, the solemn face of Pastor Libby—locked itself into my memory like a permanent bookmark.
She was gone.
And we were left behind, scrambling to reconstruct a family that could never truly fit back together again.
Chapter 17: From the fire back to the Pan – Linda & Wooten HS.
Return to the Fold
The death of my mother wasn’t just a tragedy—it was a tectonic shift. Like an aftershock that never stopped rumbling, it forced all the pieces of my life into new, unfamiliar configurations. With nowhere else to go, I returned to my father’s household—rejoining a family that had grown and evolved without me. My brother had already moved back during his sophomore year, and now I was stepping back into a dynamic that included not just him and my dad, but Linda and my stepsister, Katherine.
It was like being slotted back into a machine I was no longer calibrated for.
New School, Old Pain
With the move came another displacement: I transferred from Magruder High School to Thomas S. Wootton High School in Rockville, Maryland, barely two weeks into the ninth grade. The shift was jarring. Magruder had just begun to feel like a foundation—solid, promising. Wootton was more like shifting sand. And it wasn’t just emotional turmoil; it was academic.
Despite the chaos, I managed to hold on to one thread of continuity: football. I remained a starting offensive tackle on Wootton’s JV team, but even that couldn’t anchor me. My grades plummeted. I found myself adrift in an academic sea of high expectations and early personal disconnection.
Lab Partner from the Future
One standout memory from this time was an honors science class that paired me with a student who might as well have stepped out of WarGames. He had a home computer—an uncommon luxury in the mid-1980s—and it showed. His lab reports were pristine, professionally printed, formatted like NASA mission documentation. Mine, handwritten and clunky by comparison, felt like caveman etchings.
But it wasn’t just jealousy—it was a glimpse of the future. Even then, I knew computers would be the key to everything. That guy had a head start, and if I had to guess today? He’s probably reverse engineering propulsion systems out in the Nevada desert.
The Weight of Transition
Between the grief, the upheaval, and the unspoken tensions in the household, it’s no surprise I began to flounder. Wootton wasn’t a reset—it was a pressure cooker. I was expected to perform as if nothing had changed, when in fact, everything had.
Still, even as I struggled, the seeds of something greater were being planted. I was learning—awkwardly and often painfully—that life doesn’t wait for you to be ready. And that sometimes, even if you’re not thriving, you’re still moving forward.
Chapter 18: Randolph-Macon Academy
  

Ron’s graduation party with Doug Ubel, Greg Gonya, Jody, ? and Me.
The Scout Path Leads to a New Chapter
Amidst personal upheaval, Scouting remained my lifeline. It grounded me in structure, fellowship, and purpose. From long hikes along the C&O Canal and the Appalachian Trail to exploring the ramparts of Fort McHenry in Baltimore, I found peace in adventure and identity in the Scout Motto—Be Prepared—a code I still live by.
It was in Boys' Life Magazine, a Scouting staple, that my destiny pivoted. There it was: a bold ad for Randolph-Macon Academy, complete with a fighter jet crest. For a kid dreaming of the Air Force Academy, it was a beacon. That moment lit the fuse.
Tragedy Funds Opportunity
After my mother’s death, her Social Security survivor benefits were redirected to support my education. As fate would have it, those monthly checks matched RMA's tuition almost exactly. With the rising tension at home—fueled by my stepmother’s coldness and my stepsister’s growing distance—it became more than a possibility. It became necessary.
Transferring to Randolph-Macon Academy (RMA) was a pivotal decision shaped by my need for a structured environment, a desire that grew from personal challenges and was deepened by the loss of my mother. RMA offered an Air Force JROTC program, rigorous college preparatory courses, and the boarding school experience I sought, located 86 miles west of Washington, D.C.
Structure and Ascendance
My time at RMA was transformative. I thrived academically, especially during my sophomore and junior years. Motivated by an agreement with my father to earn music CDs for good grades, I poured myself into my studies and crushed it—achieving a straight A GPA both years. I broke the bank. The deal eventually fell through—adding to the growing list of things I'd have to learn to forgive. But forgive I have. No need to carry a mountain around on my shoulders—and when I put it down, I transformed.
Brotherhood in Uniform
Among the many friendships I forged, Chuck Clark remained a constant. But at RMA, I added a new soul brother: James Garrison. A day cadet, James didn’t live in the dorms, but his home became my weekend refuge. We bonded through football and fishing—he was the Fish Whisperer, somehow always reeling in the biggest bass from his secret spots around Warrenton, Virginia.
We didn’t always get along. Fights happened. One in band class, another on the football field. James had the advantage—6'5" to my 6'1", and heavier—but it never broke us. Just boys being boys, blood brothers in the trenches.
Band of Brothers
Captain Ed Richards, the larger-than-life leader of RMA’s marching band, left an unforgettable impression. He was the heart and soul of the band, and the enforcer when tensions flared. More than once, his massive hands yanked James off me or pulled me out of a scrap. He was fair, fierce, and respected—one of the truest men I've ever known.
Mr. Barr, my math teacher for multiple years, had a sense of humor so dry it could make Douglas Adams look sentimental. I deeply respected him. Receiving the National Math Honors Scholar Award junior year wasn't just about achievement—it was about earning his nod. That mattered.
There was Mr. Ruis, who played the school organ and taught chemistry with mole conversions and dry wit. Mr. Lamb, who taught government like it was satire and served as senior class advisor. Coach Bono was less effective with Spanish conjugations but masterful with a whistle—his lessons came through tackles and pins. And then there was Mrs. Cowan, biology teacher and the very visual reason I joined the track team. Enough said.
Classmates Who Mattered
Foster Murphy and Clay Sampson were my trumpet-line rivals. Foster, who would go on to VMI, had that straight-line-to-success kind of aura. Clay, the son of a WWII P-51 ace who once flew with George H.W. Bush, could memorize a song after hearing it twice. Brilliant guy. In our trio, Foster was first chair, Clay second, and I held it down in third.
Greg Gents and Phil Starling were my senior-year crew on B1st. We snuck smokes in the staff apartment bathroom and bonded over smuggled drinks and dumb pranks. We'd sneak around the main building at night, dodging the security guard like we were in Ocean’s Eleven: Prep School Edition. We thought we were untouchable.
Misfire of a Senior Prank
Then came the prank that cost us. I wasn’t involved—but that didn’t matter. Someone thought it would be hilarious to dust the dining hall ceiling fans with pepper. During breakfast—while Colonel Meith sat front and center—the fans flipped on. Instant chaos. A choking pepper cloud descended like napalm. The entire senior class got forty tours. It sounded funny in theory. In practice? Not so much.
A Dream Formed, A Dream Derailed
With the Air Force Academy in my sights, I poured everything into that dream. Every action, every study session, every mile run was aimed at that goal. But life throws curveballs. And mine? It came hard, right at the start of senior year. A story for another chapter—but suffice to say, RMA was the place I sharpened my edge. I found structure, resilience, and the unshakeable core that would carry me through everything that followed.

Chapter 19: Rehoboth Beach, DE
Summers That Shaped Me
Each summer break from RMA carved new lessons into me. The summer between freshman and sophomore year started with Kelly Landscaping Company, thanks to a referral from my brother, who’d worked with them previously. My green 48-inch John Deere walk-behind mower was my pride and joy. I’d jog behind it through the heat and humidity, cutting huge swaths of grass at apartment complexes around Gaithersburg and North Potomac.
The following summer—between sophomore and junior year—I worked briefly for a fly-by-night contractor out of Mississippi, helping frame houses in a new development near my dad’s place in North Potomac. There were no cranes, no planning—just brute force. I carried plywood sheets and hand-lifted every single truss onto two roofs. The Mississippi crew called me "Bigun," and while they lit up joints and talked big, I did the grunt work. They stiffed me on my final check, flunked inspection, and vanished. It was a hard but essential lesson: when you get screwed, you’re on your own.
Learning to Fence
Later that summer, I walked onto the Quince Orchard High School construction site and met Jerry Little, owner of Shore Fence Company in Rehoboth Beach, Delaware. I worked for him the rest of that summer, installing miles of chain-link fencing around basketball and tennis courts and baseball fields. It was tough work, but it taught me life skills—how to sweat for a living, and how much labor goes into the stuff we take for granted.
Beach Life: Freedom and Firsts
Jerry invited me to Rehoboth Beach for the following summer. He said I could live at his mother’s strict men-only boarding house and have a blast. I was in. After my junior year, I packed my gear—including my Onkyo stereo system and JBL L60T speakers—and my dad dropped me off at the Eastern Shore.
As luck would have it, Chuck—my old Scouting and Redland buddy—was right across the street. Our friendship had its ups and downs, but hey, the guy had two Mustangs, including a black GT convertible, so it was pretty easy to rekindle things.
And what do you do with your first taste of freedom? You go skating, of course. I’d ride my Motomarina Raven 49cc moped, gifted from Dave, to the roller hockey rink twice a week. That was my first taste of team-based wheeled sports—and as expected, I was rudderless but alive.
Kim, Ocean City NJ, and the Breakdown
One night after skating, I stopped at a beachside amusement park and met Kim D'Oria, tall, striking, and vacationing in Bethany Beach. For a week, I rode my moped 45 minutes each way, blasting Hotel California on my tape player, to visit her. It was romantic and pure.
Later, she invited me to Ocean City, New Jersey. Jerry, my boss, let me borrow one of his old Chevy S10 flatbed trucks for the trip—generous, but risky. Somewhere near the Cape May ferry, it broke down. I was stranded with a football gear bag doubling as luggage and no cash. I waited out a midnight thunderstorm in a glass bus shelter with strangers, soaked and miserable. That memory’s stuck with me ever since.
The Boardwalk, Heather, and Unspoken Things
As the summer continued, I met Heather Killinger from Cleona, PA. We spent many nights walking the boardwalk, sharing stories, and occasionally more. One night at 3:30 a.m., a patrolling officer interrupted us in a compromising moment. What shocked us more wasn’t the cop—it was what we found when we came up from the beach: a crowd of silent men, moving up and down the boardwalk like phantoms. No sounds, just pairing off and disappearing into the night. Rehoboth Beach, as we learned, is a mix of powerful politicians’ hideaways and a discreet haven for gay culture.
Dave Arrives, and So Does the Party
About a month in, I called my second mom, Barbara, at Dave’s house. I told her I was having a great time building fences and building character. Coincidentally, Dave had just wrecked her car. She figured a summer at the beach would be the perfect corrective experience for him too—and she wasn’t wrong.
Barbara drove Dave down the following weekend. His first words?
"Let’s get some beer."
Thankfully, my roommates were over 21 and didn’t mind hooking us up. Armed with wine coolers and cheap beer, we hit the sand. One deserted beach house between Rehoboth and Dewey Beach became our unofficial hangout, complete with porch parties, dumb jokes, and new friendships.
Chuck, Dave, and I made that boardwalk ours. We met girls. We chased freedom. We burned memories into our bones that would last into adulthood.
Chapter 20: Football and The Car Accident
Victory, Then Violence
The car accident that happened after our first victorious football game of my senior year marked a painful turning point in my life. Oddly, I had never consciously linked that event with the game itself—until now. But the symmetry is there: my mother’s suicide had followed my first-ever high school football game, and now, years later, another life-altering event struck on the heels of another football milestone. These echoes in time seem more than coincidence.
Our senior season at Randolph-Macon Academy was off to a triumphant start. Under Coach Bono—our wrestling coach turned head football coach—we had shifted away from traditional weight training toward a brutal regimen inspired by Herschel Walker’s legendary bodyweight program. Our days were filled with relentless wind sprints, sled pushes, and bear crawls up the hills behind the practice field, where the ghosts of Civil War soldiers seemed to linger in the long grass. The field sat in the shadow of the now-defunct Avtex Fibers plant, later designated a Superfund site. Looking back, I sometimes wonder if the wind carried more than just the scent of history.
Coach Bono’s leadership brought an energy that had been missing the year before. My junior year under Coach Bronx had been demoralizing; we lost all but one game. But now, under Bono, we only lost one—flipping the script entirely. That opening win against a team whose name escapes me now wasn’t just a morale booster—it was a prophecy that we had turned the page.
The Brotherhood of the Field
That day, James Garrison and I both had reason to celebrate. James, my teammate, bandmate, and close friend, had become like a brother to me over the course of three years. We first met during football camp as sophomores and played brass instruments in the marching band—me on trumpet, him on trombone. Both of us had picked up our instruments in fourth grade, and they remained a shared language.
Though James was a day cadet and I boarded at RMA, our friendship deepened through weekends spent hiking and fishing in the woods of Warrenton, Virginia. James was what I’d call a “Fish Whisperer.” Every time we hiked into one of his secret fishing holes, he would land a monster bass like clockwork. No bragging, no pretense—just skill and instinct.
He was also an experienced spelunker. I followed him once into a cave system where the passages narrowed into nightmarish squeezes. I still have dreams—claustrophobic, breathless—of being stuck in those gaps. James, of course, slithered through them like it was nothing. At 6'5", he moved with the grace of someone half his size.

The Girls, the Quarry, the Crash
After our win, James and I decided to spend time with three girls from our school: Jennifer L., Monique Quillian, and Natalie W. They had town pass, and I had arranged to meet them in Front Royal. Monique and I had been close for some time, and I remember her hinting that the girl I was currently seeing might not be the right one—that maybe she felt something for me too. Nothing came of it, but the memory stuck.
We drove out to a rock quarry and went swimming—clothes and all. It was spontaneous and fun, like so many high school moments are, until they aren’t.
On the way back, James was driving his Pontiac Bonneville. He was inexperienced and overconfident, and as we curved down a steep embankment, he lost control. He slammed on the brakes—an instinctive but fatal mistake in a car without ABS. The vehicle careened off the road and rolled down the hill at around 50 mph.
I wasn’t wearing a seatbelt. Instinct kicked in—I braced myself with my arms, just like we practiced in football. Everything moved in slow motion. I thought I was going to die. And then I felt something strange in my shorts—black spandex biker shorts, to be exact. I thought I had broken something—or worse. But no, it was just a particularly large turd, the product of shock. Somehow, nobody noticed, and for that small grace, I am eternally grateful.
We climbed out, battered and shaken. We found a house nearby, and the family graciously let me shower off the worst of my embarrassment. Two ambulances arrived to treat the girls, who had sustained significant injuries—mostly facial, from being tossed around the cabin. James, oddly enough, had part of his nose nearly sheared off but barely bled. It was bizarre, and I still don’t fully understand it.
Discipline and Disappointment
We had obtained permission for our town pass, but the girls had not. That made all the difference. The fallout was swift and merciless. I was suspended, stripped of my National Honor Society membership, and demoted from my hard-won rank of Flight Commander.
But worst of all, my dream of attending the U.S. Air Force Academy—something I had worked toward for years—disintegrated overnight. Despite receiving two Congressional nominations from Representative Beverly Byron, my file was effectively dead on arrival after the incident.
It was soul-crushing. I had dedicated my entire high school life to that goal. I lived and breathed it. But just like that, I was out. The dominoes fell, and they didn't stop.
Life Goes On (Sort Of)
Back in Riverview, Maryland, where my father lived, my next-door neighbor was a proud Air Force Academy graduate. He was quick to remind me of it whenever the chance arose. His wife had gone to Stanford, and their combined pedigree was something they wielded like a club. I didn't begrudge them their accomplishments—well, maybe a little. It was hard not to.
Despite all that, there were still bright moments. I dated Brandy Ketchum, a local girl from Front Royal. It wasn’t built to last—she didn’t even have a private phone line—but it brought a little light into my life. I also spent time with Jeannie Strong, my sister’s best friend. We had a real connection, but distance and time pulled us apart.
RMA's female population was small—like really small. Maybe 10% of the senior class. So high school dating wasn’t exactly a booming enterprise. Most of us just focused on grades, football, and surviving the system. There was something military about it all—formal, structured, and relentless.
The Echoes That Remain
Looking back now, I see that night not just as a tragedy or turning point, but as a hinge in the narrative arc of my life. Everything that followed—college, the Navy, my career in software—unfolded from that moment like the branching paths of a quantum timeline.
It’s tempting to wish for a rewind button. To buckle that seatbelt. To skip the quarry. To play it safe. But that’s not how life works. You take the hits, you clean up, you walk away if you’re lucky—and I was lucky.
If nothing else, that night taught me this: sometimes surviving is enough.
Chapter 21: My sister’s friends …
Navigating high school romance at Randolph-Macon Academy was anything but typical. With a male-to-female ratio that could generously be described as lopsided, dating within the cadet corps often felt more like a fantasy than a reality. For many of us, meaningful relationships came not from within the academy walls, but from the extended networks that surrounded us—family friends, community connections, and, in my case, my sister’s closest companions.
That’s how I met Jeannie Strong, a friend of my sister's who would become one of the earliest loves of my life.
What began as a warm friendship blossomed into something deeper. There was a sweetness and a sincerity in Jeannie that grounded me during those chaotic high school years. Her visits, marked by long conversations and shared laughter, brought a sense of normalcy and peace that was otherwise absent in the regimented boarding school environment.
Jeannie’s father, a highly respected graduate of the United States Naval Academy, added a layer of fascination to our connection. He had flown F-4 Phantoms in Vietnam and regaled us with stories that painted vivid portraits of life at Mach speeds—tales laced with wisdom and humor, like his firm belief that “with enough thrust, anything can fly.”
I remember walking with Jeannie through the tree-lined paths near the National Geographic headquarters in Washington, D.C. The serenity of that place became the backdrop to many of our conversations—about life, about trauma, and about growing up in a world that often moved too fast. It was during one of those walks that Jeannie confided in me about a deeply personal and painful experience from her past. Her willingness to share that vulnerability not only brought us closer, but also helped me develop a deeper empathy for the hidden pain that many carry.
  

With Jeanie Strong, Homecoming Sophomore Year 1987
Our relationship, like many young loves, faded over time—not due to any dramatic falling out, but simply because life kept moving. The distance between our homes, the lack of personal phones, and the weight of our separate ambitions gradually carved a quiet divide. Jeannie would go on to become a successful attorney and a devoted mother. I look back at our time together with nothing but gratitude—thankful to have played even a small part in her remarkable journey.
  

Jeanie with Katherine, ~1989
And then there was Sharna, my sister’s other best friend.
My connection with Sharna evolved differently. During my senior year at RMA, her family moved to Warrenton, and she remained in touch with me even after my mother’s passing. That year, I often spent weekends at her home, a haven of normalcy and quiet friendship that felt like family. Our bond was deeply affectionate, but never romantic—something I describe as “Ted Lasso love”: warm, earnest, and firmly platonic, though filled with genuine care.
But as life does, it looped back.
Years later, during my time at Nuclear Prototype training in Clifton Park, New York, our paths crossed again. This time, our connection reignited into something more intense and emotionally charged. It was brief, but meaningful—and ultimately, I failed her. The details of my lapse aren’t as important as the fact that I never had the chance to say what I needed to say: I was sorry.
When I eventually tried to reach her, I discovered that I had lost my father’s old black address book, which contained the only phone number I had for her. It was a small but profound loss—one that carried with it a lifetime of what-ifs. I never found her again, and that absence became a quiet ache I carried for years.
Time has taught me the value of forgiveness—of others, but especially of myself. And in the absence of reconciliation, I’ve learned to make peace with the past by embracing the lesson, not the regret.
Chapter 22: Mountain State University to Orlando NTC
A Frostburg Beginning
After graduating high school, I embarked on my collegiate journey with a full academic scholarship to Frostburg State University, located in the scenic mountains of Western Maryland—an institution that also inspired Greg Garcia of "My Name is Earl." Embracing the life of an engineering major, I became part of the rugby team, diving into a lively social scene. However, the structured discipline and warmer climate of Orlando, the home of Naval Nuclear Power School, presented a compelling contrast, motivating me to join the Navy after my first semester.
The Pontiac 6000: Lessons in Responsibility
Graduating high school brought an interesting twist: my parents, striving for a sense of fairness, bestowed upon me their aging, puke-green Pontiac 6000 Station Wagon. This was the same vehicle that Linda, my stepmother, had driven for the better part of a decade before it was ceremoniously handed down to me. It may seem petty to note—given the privileges I already had—that during our high school years, my stepsister Katherine received a Nissan NX1600, which she eventually totaled in an accident, the details of which remained a mystery to me. The aftermath of her accident was met with what I perceived as a reward: she was given a reliable Saturn SL2 and took over the entire basement area of our house, a domain where I became unwelcome.
I went on to equip this Pontiac 6000 with a robust custom stereo system, marking the beginning of a series of expertly configured 12-volt car audio installations and the continuation of my passion for loud, high-fidelity music. Regrettably, I was oblivious to the importance of regular vehicle maintenance, most critically, oil changes. On a solitary drive back to Frostburg from the local skating rink, the car abruptly died, and the engine refused to restart. A local mechanic, nestled in the steep valleys of the surrounding area, managed to replace the defunct engine with a used one for $795.00.
Also worth mentioning is that upon my graduation, my grandparents Vassie gave me a considerable number of immature savings bonds. The intention behind this generous gift was to teach me the value of saving and how it could multiply one's wealth. However, lacking financial foresight, I quickly depleted these bonds to pay for the new engine and to indulge in wedge-shaped, Philly-style pizzas from a favorite local eatery.
Influences and the Decision to Enlist
During this time, I had a significant conversation with Pete Linzmeyer, my senior-year roommate from RMA, who had recently returned from Army boot camp. His description of the demanding military life resonated with me, reinforcing my decision to enlist. Separately, my brother’s high school friend Charlie Benson had recently graduated from Nuclear Power School and gave me the low down on the challenges, and rewards, of enlisting in the Navy as a Nuclear operator.
In parallel to these academic and military aspirations, I faced a personal challenge with weight management. A short stint as a pizza delivery driver led to a rapid weight gain—from 195 to 235 pounds. The Navy provided a stark change in routine, and by the end of boot camp, I had shed a remarkable 60 pounds. This cycle of weight loss and gain was not new to me; during my junior and senior years of high school wrestling, I experienced similar fluctuations. This ongoing battle with weight is a thread that weaves through the narrative of my life, a consistent struggle that endures.
My time at Frostburg State University was a period of real despondency. I remember the excitement of arriving at the school and unpacking my giant stereo system, consisting of a mid-level Onkyo Tuner Amplifier, a sophisticated Rube Goldberg 6-disc changer Onkyo component CD player, an equally sophisticated matched Onkyo dual tape deck, and JBL L60T speakers. I had always been an audiophile with an enormous collection of CDs spanning a broad range of tastes, including Kitaro, Yanni, Yes, Yaz, The Cure, Depeche Mode, Ministry, Nitzer Ebb, Guns N’ Roses, Metallica, John Williams and the Boston Pops, the Glenn Miller Orchestra, Jazz, Classical, Nature Sounds, Deep Forest, and early dance hits like Dance Mix USA, which would evolve into my passion for EDM, among other things— notably how DJ Doboy's Vocal Editions would become one of the most popular genres of music today.
Rugby
And then there was rugby. I don't know why I didn't try to walk onto the football team. I didn't have or even attempt to obtain an athletic scholarship, but one thing that I think would have changed the entire trajectory of my life would have been just to walk onto the team and give it my best shot. Instead, partly because it was the only social grouping I could join in my freshman semester—Frostburg outlawed freshman fraternity rushing—I joined the Rugby Team. So, I paid my $45.00 dues and in exchange obtained a cool rugby jersey and, more importantly, a free pass to daily drinking events. The rugby team maintained a party house just off campus, and I recall many a drunken afternoon shouting Bob Marley's "Don't worry..." with the whole team.
Shoot the Boot!
In one memorable episode at the local restaurant we regularly frequented together, one of the senior members challenged me to a dare: drink from his boot. This was disgusting, but I was super intoxicated and not one to back down from a dare. The senior member of the team took off his boot, and to cheers of "Shoot the Boot! Shoot the Boot!" I guzzled down a bootful of beer. At the house a short time later, this motif of drinking from disgusting things was replicated. The same team member challenged me to drink from the plunger. This was thoroughly disgusting, and at first, I was like, "NO F'ING WAY!!" But then the senior took the plunger, topped it off from the keg, and proceeded to down the whole thing. Then triumphantly, he handed a topped-off plunger to me and, with steely eyes, commanded, "DRINK!" I had no choice and downed it to the cheers of my teammates and Bob Marley wailing in the background.
Playing Rugby Against the Naval Academy!
Ironically, my physical fitness dropped off rapidly during this time. I experienced tremendous weight gain even though I avidly skated the hilly campus as regularly as I could several days a week. I recall in particular one rugby match where we played the US Naval Academy's C squad rugby team. Their C squad, meaning they had two better teams than this! They cleaned our clocks, but in the end, we all partied together that night, and fortunately for the Naval Academy lads, our female rugby team provided what I can only imagine was more intimate care than we did on the
A small but notable part of my college life was joining the school's intramural hockey team. I owned a pair of Bauer hockey skates, which I had previously used only during public skating sessions at different ice rinks. Although I had some experience playing organized roller hockey in Rehoboth Beach, Delaware, two summers prior, I had never played organized ice hockey before. Regrettably, my memories of these games are marred by breathlessness and frustration; by the time I joined the team, I had become significantly overweight and my cardiovascular fitness was poor, which greatly hindered my performance on the ice.
Adventures with Dave Laime
On Halloween Weekend, Dave Laime, who I talked to frequently through this time, gave me a call one Thursday afternoon and asked me if I wanted to go on a road trip. Without needing to check my calendar, I enthusiastically accepted. About six hours later, he arrived in Frostburg with his mom's trusty 1980's vintage Honda Civic and with barely $40.00's between us, hit the road for Columbus, Ohio where a girl he had met at the beach the prior summer was attending Ohio State University. So we drove the whole way, smoking generic cigarettes, singing along to Ball and Chain and Story of My Life by Social Distortion all the way. We picked up his girlfriend, then headed down to Athens for the renowned OU Halloween observances where we met up with his girlfriend's girlfriend and enjoyed the festivities. On a separate weekend, we went to Radford and Virginia Tech for a notable weekend of youthfull fun and somewhere else to ODU in Richmond.
Through it all, my fondness for cars remained a constant source of joy. Even my puke-green Pontiac 6000 Station Wagon was an oasis of sorts, its impressive stereo system transforming each delivery into a mini-concert experience. This car, and later traded for a $14,500.00 red 1990 Geo Storm GSI, were more than just transportation; they were sanctuaries on wheels, complete with all the shiny buttons and features that brought me happiness, no matter where the road took me. Nowhere else could I find the shear joy of being able to control every aspect of my environment. Too cold, dial the AC down. In a sad mood, pop in some classical music, or Depeche Mode or a Letter to Elise. On the go? How about some Ministry, Nitzereb, Thrill Kill Cult.
In the early morning hours of January 8, 1991, I was driving home from my job delivering Dominos Pizza where I coincidentally gained roughly 25 lbs, when I failed to come to a complete and full stop at a blinking red light at 1:30 AM. Fortunately, there was a vigilant police officer 'manning' this notorious intersection of death even at these early, empty hours, protecting the slumbering citizens of Montgomery County from evil stop light not stoppers. I'm just saying there wasn't a car on the road in any direction. The officer who pulled me over noted that there had been a number of fatal accidents at this intersection and she felt it was her duty to correct my abhorant behavior. Thus I recieved my first moving violation which required a tremendous amount of paperwork the next day at MEPS and ultimately ended up costing me at least 10 K of increased insurance premiums.
Chapter 23: Bootcamp and NTC Orlando
Departure from Frostburg
On January 9, 1991, I left Frostburg, Maryland, behind and drove to my recruiter’s office. From there, we took a General Motors government vehicle to MEPS (Military Entrance Processing Station) in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. I was accompanied by a few other recruits, including a female who would later go on to corpsman school. She and I stayed in contact, and our paths would cross again years later when I was stationed in Norfolk.
MEPS: The Bureaucratic Gauntlet
That day at MEPS was consumed by medical exams, paperwork, and waivers—including ones for my second-grade psychiatric hold at 4 Orange in Washington Children’s Hospital and a recent moving violation that nearly cost me my enlistment. It was all business, all day.
The night before we officially processed, the Navy put us up in a mid-tier motel. I still remember talking and flirting with that same female recruit, sharing the electric anticipation of stepping into a new life. In the end, she picked someone else, but we kept in touch. That brief connection, and the unspoken nervous excitement among the group, made the experience feel almost cinematic.
Welcome to RTC Orlando
The flight to Orlando is a blur. What I remember with total clarity is the humiliation of the urinalysis upon arrival. We were herded into a gymnasium-sized room with masking tape on the tile floor, lined up like cattle, and ordered to pee while staff watched from behind. I froze. Despite repeated commands, jabs, and marching drills to “encourage” performance, I couldn’t go. My personal pee shyness kicked in hard. It took me hours of pacing, hydration, and shame before I could finally deliver a sample—at approximately 3:30 AM.
My reward? Barely an hour of sleep before being jolted awake by drill instructors hurling a metal trash can down the center of the barracks. Welcome to Navy Boot Camp.
Wax On, Wax Off – Navy Style
What makes Navy Boot Camp unique isn’t just the physical training, but its obsession with detail. Locker folding was an art form. Each garment had to be pressed, aligned, and folded to precise measurements—basically military origami. Then came Locker Drills, where we were ordered to dump every perfectly folded item onto the deck and refold it under timed conditions while instructors barked corrections.
Every moment had purpose. Even the language had to change. We didn’t walk—we marched. We didn’t drink water—we drank from the scuttlebutt. We cleaned the deck, stowed our gear in lockers, and stood silently next to the bulkhead. We weren't in a hallway—we were in a passageway. The Navy has its own dictionary, and by the end of week one, you’d better be fluent.
The Grinder and Galley
Every day, we assembled on The Grinder—a massive slab of concrete where we practiced marching, performed PT, and waited to be marched to meals. Compared to the robotic precision of Air Force JROTC, Navy marching style had swagger. That swagger was encouraged and reinforced during cadence drills, where we added a bounce and rhythm foreign to my old RMA habits.
Each morning, we stood motionless in ranks, inching forward across the Grinder toward the galley. Once inside, we waited silently. Meals were quiet, efficient, and completely joyless. I refused to eat. Whether it was depression, disgust, or just the way my body processed stress, I literally ate nothing for the first four weeks. I likely lost 30 pounds during that time.
The “Fat Boy” Program
When I arrived at boot camp, I weighed in at a husky 235 pounds—largely the result of my pizza delivery phase and sedentary lifestyle prior to enlistment. This put me squarely into what we informally called the “fat boy program.” The official designation didn’t matter much—everyone called it that. The deal was simple: do extra PT voluntarily on your own time, before the rest of the company was even awake, or risk falling behind in the Navy’s physical readiness standards.
So, every morning at 0330, I was up and on the Grinder—the enormous slab of concrete where we trained and assembled. Alone under the floodlights, I pounded out sets of push-ups and sit-ups, followed by a five-mile run around the perimeter. It was grueling and lonely, but it worked. The rest of boot camp had its scheduled PT, of course, but it wasn’t consistent or intense enough on its own to get me back into Navy shape.
By the time I graduated boot camp, I had lost 65 pounds, dropping from 235 down to 170 pounds. I needed an entirely new set of uniforms, as the original ones I’d been issued practically hung off me. I’d slimmed down so much that people I’d trained alongside for weeks didn’t recognize me at graduation.
The 50 State Flag Team
Somewhere amid the early-morning PT, the folding drills, and the constant pressure, I was selected to represent our company on the 50 State Flag Team. RTC Orlando had two ceremonial units: the Unarmed Drill Team and the 50 State Flag Team. The flag team was especially sought after for one important reason—it was the only activity that allowed male and female recruits to train together. In a sea of gender-segregated barracks, PT sessions, and day-to-day duties, this was a major social opportunity.
Each day, I would muster with the flag team for marching rehearsals, precise formations, and ceremonial duties. It gave me a mental break from the monotony of boot camp and the pressure of inspections and drills. But more importantly, it was during this time that I met a girl I genuinely liked—smart, poised, grounded. We clicked in a way that felt rare in the high-stress, highly controlled boot camp environment.
We never officially dated—boot camp rules made that impossible—and after graduation, life moved us in different directions. She joined me and my family for a day at Universal Studios during liberty following graduation, and we took a few incredible photographs together, full of optimism and light. But as was often the case in my life at the time, the thread of connection was severed prematurely—my father lost his black address book, the only place where her contact information was written. No phone number, no last name committed to memory. She became one of those perfect “what if” chapters that closes before the next page even begins.
  

Mind Games and the Line
Boot camp wasn't just physical—it was deeply psychological. The instructors were masters of the mind game, and one particular evening stands out like a training scar etched into memory. Chief Whitehead and our First Class Instructor marched in just after evening chow. We knew something was coming—their expressions had that telltale “we’re about to break you” look.
This time, it was a locker drill.
We’d been through dozens by now. Dump everything on the floor, fold it back perfectly to spec, get it inspected, then start over. This time, though, there was a twist. Chief Whitehead stood front and center and laid it out:
“This is going to be the most brutal inspection yet. If anything is out of place—one jot, one iota—you’ll be dropped to do eight-count bodybuilders until you collapse. Then we’ll reset, and you’ll do it again. And again. So, if you’re not absolutely confident, save yourself the shame—step to the outside of the bunks.”
The barracks had two rows of bunks, lockers on the inside facing a tile line where we were expected to place our toes. That line was the crucible—the commitment. Step off, and you were spared... or so it seemed. Stand firm, and you faced the wrath of the most intense locker inspection imaginable.
It was clearly a trap—a confidence drill, a test of mental fortitude. I saw it immediately. I stayed on the line. One by one, others peeled away. Even our Recruit Company Commander stepped off, eyes down, pride evaporated. In the end, only four of us out of 82 recruits remained, standing silently with our toes on that line.
Chief Whitehead looked us over with a sly grin and said:
“This is it? These are the only ones who think they’re ready?”
Then, with a dramatic pause:
“Alright, you four—at ease. Everyone else? Get into the Lounge.”
The Lounge was a relic from a forgotten era—a multipurpose room tucked behind the barracks that we’d never actually used except for this moment. It was massive when empty, but with 78 recruits crammed in, it became a furnace of misery. From our racks, we could hear them: the moans, the groans, the nonstop PT. Push-ups. Sit-ups. Running in place. Eight-count bodybuilders until the walls sweated along with them.
For two hours, the pain echoed off the tile floors and cinder block walls. It was glorious. Not because of their suffering—but because we’d passed the test. The point of the exercise was never locker perfection—it was mental toughness, belief in yourself under pressure, even when surrounded by fear and doubt. And the line... the line had separated those who folded from those who didn’t.
It was one of the proudest moments of boot camp for me.
Graduation and a Family United
Upon graduating from boot camp, there was a rare and moving coming-together moment in my family—a moment of pride that transcended the usual tensions and fractures. Among the attendees was the man who had inspired me more than perhaps anyone else: my grandfather, Harry B. Vassie.
Seeing him there, standing at attention as we marched in formation, filled me with immense pride. This was no ordinary man—he was a World War II veteran who had served as an airdales crewman aboard the legendary CV-5 USS Yorktown, a ship that played a pivotal role in the Battle of Midway, one of the most decisive victories in naval history.
His service during the war was only the beginning. After the conflict, he earned a college degree and returned to the Navy, this time as an officer in Naval Intelligence. Later, he transitioned into one of the most secretive arms of the U.S. government—the National Security Agency (NSA)—where he worked through the height of the Cold War. To this day, the exact nature of his service at the NSA remains shrouded in mystery. True to the values he held sacred, my grandfather never broke his oath of secrecy, not even near the end of his life.
A few short months after my graduation, he passed away. He never spoke about the classified details of his intelligence work, but he did let me know, with unmistakable clarity, how proud he was of me for continuing the family’s tradition of service. His stoic integrity, quiet brilliance, and deep sense of duty left a mark on me that has never faded.
My decision to join the Navy wasn’t just a reaction to life circumstances—it was a direct legacy of the man who had stood tall through one of history’s most perilous conflicts, who had moved silently through corridors of secrets in defense of the nation, and who had modeled what it meant to serve with honor and discretion.
Standing there at RTC Orlando, the Navy's version of graduation caps flying in the Florida sun, I didn’t just feel like a new recruit. I felt like I had stepped into the current of something larger than myself—a continuation of my grandfather’s story, and perhaps one day, a torch I might pass on.
  

A-School and a New Chapter in Orlando
After the formalities of boot camp and the deeply moving family reunion at graduation, I began the next phase of my Navy journey: Machinist’s Mate “A” School at Naval Training Center Orlando. The program was 16 weeks of intense, nuclear-focused mechanical training—grueling in pace and precision. It was here that we were expected to learn the language of machines, and by the end of it, operate and maintain the vital propulsion systems of America’s most advanced naval vessels.
Success in A-School came with its rewards. Upon graduation, I was automatically promoted to Machinist's Mate 3rd Class (MM3), which placed me at E-4 on the pay scale. This wasn’t just symbolic—it was financial. Unlike E-3s, E-4s were eligible for sea pay and submarine pay, a detail that might seem minor but would become relevant in the months to follow.
Amber and the Geo Storm
It was also during this chapter—somewhere between classroom drills and cross-sectional schematics—that I had a fateful encounter at a stoplight on Colonial Boulevard in Orlando, sitting behind the wheel of my red 1990 Geo Storm GSI. My car bore a Just Blow Me Surfwax Bubble Gum sticker on the side—irreverent, sure, but somehow a conversation starter. That’s how I met Amber Goettig.
Amber and I hit it off almost instantly. We dated for about six months, and during that time, she became a semi-permanent resident at my grandparents' house in Palm Harbor, which sat on a canal off Lake Tarpon. My grandmother didn’t seem to mind—at least not outwardly—and we slipped into a kind of routine that felt, at times, almost domestic.
  

Funeral and Family Crossroads
Amber was by my side when my grandfather passed away just months after attending my boot camp graduation. She stood with me at the funeral, a difficult and heavy day that carried the weight of both grief and legacy. That she was present meant something, even if what came next left an unexpected impression on the family.
During the post-service gathering, Amber rode with my Aunt Abbey in the family limo. I wasn't there for the conversation that unfolded, but later, Abbey pulled me aside and quietly expressed concern. Apparently, Amber had shared stories of early childhood abuse, deeply personal and unsettling details that my aunt felt were both inappropriate for the occasion and indicative of deeper wounds.
“Choose your girlfriends more carefully,” she told me, her tone firm but not unkind. It was jarring advice—uninvited but not untrue. At the time, I didn’t know what to make of it. I was young, emotionally scattered, and still trying to piece together who I was as a sailor, a man, and a son in mourning.
That moment planted a seed—a lesson in boundaries, maturity, and the complexities of carrying other people’s pain when you're still trying to process your own. Amber and I didn’t last much longer after that, but the experience remains woven into my memory of that year. Part grief, part growth.
Life Beyond the Grinder
During those formative months at Machinist’s Mate A School and later Nuclear Power School, I forged bonds with shipmates who would accompany me through the crucible of nuclear training and the early days of my fleet career. Unlike boot camp, A School came with a luxury I hadn’t known in a while: free time. We weren’t completely cut loose, but I could finally explore a bit of Orlando’s nightlife and culture.
This was during Desert Storm, and America had suddenly rediscovered its appreciation for servicemembers. Uniforms turned heads. Military IDs opened doors. We weren’t treated with suspicion or indifference—we were seen, often quite literally, as heroes.
VIP at Universal Studios
One of the most surreal perks during this time was free VIP access to the newly opened Universal Studios Orlando. Just flash your military ID at the gate and in return you'd get unlimited access, front-of-the-line privileges, and even backstage entry to most attractions. For a broke, wide-eyed E-3, it felt like cheating—like you’d stumbled into some glitch in the simulation where you suddenly mattered.
Ironically, what I missed most about amusement parks were the lines. Not the wait—nobody likes that—but the opportunities. A 45-minute wait for Back to the Future meant 45 minutes of conversation with random, often beautiful, strangers. When you’re young, in shape, and wearing your "I'm in the military during a war" face, let’s just say the odds were better than even. But with VIP access, we skipped all that. Straight to the ride. No charm needed. And while that was cool, it wasn’t nearly as fun.
A Short Ride With a Blonde From King Kong
Still, one moment stands out. I was in a group of five shipmates when we got routed to the backstage entrance for the King Kong: Confrontation ride. We stood on the loading platform for a few minutes, waiting to board, when I noticed one of the Universal cast members—some mid-level guy—approaching me with a folded slip of paper. On it: a name and phone number, hastily scribbled. He mumbled, “The ride supervisor told me to give this to you.”
Looking up toward the elevated, glassed-in operator booth, I could just make out a blonde silhouette waving from behind the darkened glass. Apparently, I had made an impression.
That brief backstage glance turned into a two-week whirlwind romance—the kind that only exists in your early 20s, when time bends around hormones and possibility. We snuck in what visits we could, navigated the maze of pay phones and rigid duty schedules, but ultimately, like many things at that age, it faded.
Back then, there were no cell phones, no texting, no apps. You had one shot when you called the public pay phone in the barracks, and if someone else was using it—or ignoring your request to be summoned—you just lost that shot. And so, just like King Kong, the relationship roared briefly, climbed high, and then came tumbling down.
Chapter 24: Haze Gray and Underway
  

In October of 1992, I reported aboard the USS Baton Rouge (SSN 689), a Los Angeles-class fast attack submarine already steeped in notoriety. My assignment would stretch until my release from active duty in October 1994. At the time, the Baton Rouge still bore the scars—literal and symbolic—of its high-profile underwater collision with a Soviet submarine. We departed Norfolk, Virginia, bound for Mare Island Naval Shipyard in California on what would be her final voyage with a full crew. We ran at flank speed, no longer concerned with preserving the reactor core’s life—it was a one-way trip toward decommissioning.
The ship’s prior encounter with the Russian submarine K-276 Kostroma, during a covert surveillance operation off the coast of Severomorsk on February 11, 1992, cast a long shadow. Both subs were operating silently on passive sonar, blind to each other until the very last moment. The Kostroma, surfacing unexpectedly, struck the Baton Rouge near her stern. The impact crumpled her aft ballast tanks and left the Russian sub visibly gashed. By some miracle, there were no casualties, but the event carried consequences far beyond hull integrity. It fractured morale, and the aftershocks would ripple through the crew for months.
Following the incident, Commander JB Kolbeck assumed command. Though competent and by-the-book, his leadership was forced to contend with a ship’s crew denied the one thing that might have mended the rift: shore leave. Our long-anticipated liberty in Amsterdam—a well-earned chance for release and reconnection—was scrapped. In its place: more duty, more drills, and rising tension below decks.
Despite this, the crew powered forward. In drydock at Mare Island, we labored side by side with shipyard personnel to make the Baton Rouge whole again. Many of us pulled double shifts, taking pride in our resilience. But something had shifted. With morale low and little outlet for pressure, hazing rituals intensified, gradually twisting from rites of passage into more toxic forms of hierarchy enforcement. Tradition began to bend under strain.
Cranking in the Crawfish Inn
One of the most humbling yet unifying rituals aboard the Baton Rouge was Cranking—the Navy tradition where each enlisted sailor takes a temporary assignment as a Food Service Attendant. During my four-month tour in the galley, I learned firsthand what powered morale more than any motivational poster ever could: food. Our galley was affectionately dubbed the Crawfish Inn, and it wasn’t an ironic name.
Thanks to the submarine service’s inflated food budget, we operated more like a military-funded Golden Corral at sea—steak, lobster, French toast, and all-you-can-eat everything. The culinary spread was genuinely absurd for a warfighting machine submerged hundreds of feet beneath the ocean. It’s no surprise I gained 50 pounds in under a year, most of it while cranking. With physical activity options limited to a decrepit Stairmaster and a stationary bike that barely functioned, those extra meals stuck with me.
Equal Plates for All Ranks
Unlike surface ships, submarines don’t segregate the chow line. There’s one galley for everyone, and while officers dined in the Wardroom, they were still eating the exact same food—just plated and served for them. Our Food Service Chief, a former flag chef who had cooked for admirals, opted to bring his culinary talents underwater. His passion for cooking transformed our expectations of what military food could be.
I still remember the pizzeria-quality scratch-made pizzas, the steak dinners, and the time we served actual king crab legs. These meals weren’t just good for military food—they were good period. For once, a recruiter’s promise about "great Navy chow" wasn’t a lie.
Bucket Brigades and Submarine Storage Science
Getting underway for deployment wasn’t a simple matter of throwing pallets on board. It required a bucket brigade of sailors, shoulder to shoulder from the pier to the torpedo room, passing provisions hand to hand—hundreds of boxes, crates, and cans. There’s no loading dock on a submarine; the entire crew became logistics.
Every inch of space aboard the Baton Rouge was precious. We packed stores into every available cavity, from overheads to bilges. Some passageways were so crammed with goods that I had to duck walk just to get through them. It was a lesson in both efficiency and humility—serving meals I helped carry aboard in spaces barely wide enough to turn around in.
Friday Morning Launches: The Nuclear Wake-Up Call
During my operational year aboard the USS Baton Rouge, a rhythm developed that came to define our weeks: we left almost every Friday morning. If you were in the transit duty section, this meant showing up at 0330 sharp. As nuclear-trained personnel, our startup ritual was an intricate ballet of valves, checklists, and reactor plant procedures, culminating by about 0730 with the reactor fully heated up and ready to go.
By contrast, the “Coners”—non-nuclear personnel who occupied the forward sections of the sub—had a far simpler task. Their system startup usually involved something like flipping a switch and waiting. This contrast fueled an ongoing and deep-rooted rivalry between the nukes and the non-nukes, and between A-Gangers (conventional machinist's mates) and nuclear MMs. The jokes never ended. One that stuck with me:
"Coners get the [bleep] and nukes get the shaft."
We took it as a badge of honor.
The Irony of "Haze Gray and Underway"
I often chuckled at the not-so-empty threats made by instructors during A School, Power School, and Prototype:
“If you fail out, you’ll be haze gray and underway chipping paint.”
Well, spoiler alert: I graduated. I didn’t fail out. And yet, I still found myself haze gray, underway, and chipping a whole lot of paint.
My daily routine as a crank (Food Service Attendant) often included three to four hours of brightwork polishing in the midships passageway. It was a ritual that demanded patience, precision, and an odd sense of pride. The Navy doesn’t just want you to run a reactor—they want the stainless steel rails and trim to be so polished they can reflect your soul.
Master of the TDU Tube Turds
The real education came in the Trash Disposal Unit (TDU) room, where I developed a skill that no textbook ever covered. This was the dirty business of compacting the day’s waste into “TDU tube turds.” These compacted metal tubes were hand-assembled from flat stamped steel sheets, origami-folded into tubes with tabs that had to be bent down precisely. You'd drop in a weight, load it into the hydraulic compactor, and pull the lever—watching putrid, fermented goo ooze through the vent holes. That slime had a stench so potent it would cling to your skin, uniform, and very soul.
I routinely spent 12 to 14 hours per day in that sealed metal room, wading through the rotting waste of 140 sailors, building and loading those damn tubes, sealing them, and sending them off to their deep-sea graves. The smell was so bad it turned me into a walking biohazard, earning me a wide berth from the rest of the crew no matter where I went.
TDU Day: The Trash Ceremony of the Deep
TDU Day aboard the USS Baton Rouge wasn’t just about waste disposal—it was practically a ceremonial affair. After rising to periscope depth, a coner chief would descend to the TDU room to supervise the ritual. Communication was routed through sound-powered phones, and once we had a solid link with control, we’d begin the sequence.
With eight or nine “TDU tube turds”—those carefully folded metal containers filled with compacted shipboard waste—loaded up like refuse torpedoes, the launch began. Each tube was dropped into the loading tray, sealed tight, and ejected into the ocean one by one with methodical, almost reverent coordination.
As strange as it sounds, this was one of the few moments in the trash detail that actually felt satisfying. It turned the drudgery of compacting garbage in a stinking hot box into something resembling a climactic release, both figuratively and literally. There’s a weird kind of pride in knowing you’ve launched something into the abyss.
A Submarine’s Early Death: Clinton’s Cuts & Baton Rouge’s Fate
When I first checked aboard the Baton Rouge, the plan was to refuel the ship during my second year. Given the expected 30+ years of service life post-refueling for a 688-class fast attack sub, this seemed like standard ops.
But politics got in the way.
In the wake of the Soviet sub collision, and with President Clinton’s campaign promises to cut defense spending, our boat was selected as the first 688-class sub to be decommissioned early. A rumor persisted among the crew—maybe half myth, maybe half-truth—that Baton Rouge was originally designated SSN 688. But due to delays in construction or scheduling mishaps, it was rebranded as SSN 689, and USS Los Angeles took the inaugural 688 hull number. That tidbit, if true, only added to the ship’s mystique and sting of early retirement.
Tight Bonds and Near Bar Fights in Puerto Rico
Despite the politics and the smell of TDU juice, service aboard the Baton Rouge forged strong bonds. I had several close shipmates: Andrew Jones, Kenneth Senn, and Louis Deleandro, to name a few. But one particular memory stands out.
While we were docked at Roosevelt Roads, Puerto Rico, on a low-key weeknight shore leave, five of us ventured to the only open bar outside base. The place was empty except for us—until five Marines walked in.
Now, if you know Marines and you know sailors, you know where this could’ve gone. These guys weren’t there to swap stories or buy us a round. They wanted to fight. And although I was the biggest in our group at 6’1”, 230 lbs, these Marines still had size, strength, and the home field advantage.
But then came Petty Officer First Class Steven Denoia. He was the head of the Electrician’s Division and, as it turned out, a master of verbal judo. With the smoothness that would later land him success in the business world, Denoia disarmed the whole situation with a few well-timed jokes and genuine friendliness. What could’ve been a full-blown brawl turned into drinks, laughter, and even a few exchanged stories. Classic military life—tense one second, unforgettable the next.
The Accidental Gift of Decommissioning
As fate would have it, being assigned to the first 688-class submarine to be decommissioned turned out to be an unexpected blessing. Because there was no formal playbook for our class, a significant part of our mission became writing the procedures ourselves.
For earlier submarines, the decommissioning process had been tightened to a 3–4 month evolution, but for us, it unfolded more like an extended, lightly-supervised sabbatical at Mare Island Naval Shipyard. The pace was slower, the expectations fuzzier, and the days—while still filled with work—left room for things like personal development, exploration, and actual rest, a luxury rarely found in submarine service.
Cross-Country Orders: A Geo Storm and $600 Freedom
And then came the cherry on top.
As a Geo Storm GSI owner and a sailor receiving a Permanent Change of Duty Station (PCS), I was given a modest but mighty $600 travel stipend and a free pass to drive across the country from Norfolk, Virginia, to Mare Island, California.
This was more than just a relocation—it was a rite of passage. A solo journey across the American continent, in a car I’d customized and loved, represented a brief chapter of true freedom. For once, the military had handed me an opportunity that didn’t come with barking commands or shipboard confinement. It came with open roads, gas stations, roadside diners, and a stereo system tuned to the soundtrack of my life.
And it was perfect.
The Thrill Kill Cult Rides Again
Life at Mare Island wasn’t all procedural manuals and shipyard monotony. In fact, it might've been the closest the Navy ever got to issuing me a vacation. Enter the Thrill Kill Cult—not the band, but our crew: Ken Senn, Andy Jones, Lou Deleandro, and yours truly. Together, we turned Northern California into our personal playground.
From bar-hopping in Napa Valley to aimless drives through the redwoods and coastal fog, we lived like we were invincible. Four freshly-cut sailors stuffed into my red Geo Storm GSI, stereo thumping, freedom echoing off the canyon walls—it was peak early-90s outlaw Americana.
Golden Gate Tollgate Mayhem
One of our more, let’s say, creative maneuvers happened near the Golden Gate Bridge. With zero foresight and exactly no toll money, I made the executive decision to reverse off the on-ramp like it was a Grand Theft Auto mission.
Enter: Sergeant Reno 911—a California highway patrol officer with sunglasses too small for his mustache and enough sass for a sitcom. The ticket he wrote? Never paid. Add it to the growing list of bad ideas that somehow made life more memorable.
  

Sacramento: More Tickets, More Laughs
Just in case our record needed padding, Sacramento came through. An illegal U-turn later, we were yet again explaining ourselves to a confused cop while trying not to laugh. We never did figure out why the U-turn was illegal—chalk it up to military logic clashing with civilian infrastructure.
But that was the nature of the Mare Island interlude: a cocktail of youth, horsepower, and just enough recklessness to make every week a story worth retelling.
Wiser (Maybe), Grateful (Definitely)
Through it all, I gained more than fines and stories. I forged lifelong friendships, experienced California’s soul, and learned a few hard-earned lessons—like maybe don’t reverse off major toll bridges, and always check local traffic laws before trusting your gut.
But mostly? I’m just grateful.
Grateful for those guys, that car, that time, and everything it taught me about loyalty, freedom, and how far a full tank and a good stereo can take you.
Five-Day Weekends and the Call of Dave
Throughout my time at Nuclear Prototype School in Ballston Spa, NY, and later while stationed aboard the USS Baton Rouge in Norfolk, I developed a survival ritual: driving to see Dave Laime. Those "five-day weekends" (which sometimes stretched a little longer than scheduled) were a lifeline. No matter how grueling the week—whether it was heat stress drills in Ballston Spa or bilge-cleaning rotations in Norfolk—I knew that if I could just get to Northern Virginia, Dave would be there, and with him, a guaranteed good time.
And Dave wasn’t just anyone. He was a force—funny, charismatic, musically brilliant, and the gravitational center of whatever orbit he entered. As I’ve mentioned elsewhere, entire volumes could be written just chronicling the adventures we shared, and still, they wouldn’t do justice to his impact on my life.
The Naked Soul Brotherhood
  

During this era, Dave was fronting a band called the Naked Soul Brothers, playing small venues across Northern Virginia with a motley crew of deeply talented musicians. Every visit home followed a pattern: hug Barbara, his amazing and eternally welcoming mom; descend into the finished basement, Dave’s personal headquarters; and map out the weekend’s conquests, both musical and social.
  

There were always house parties, always gigs, and always groupies—lots and lots of groupies. And when your best friend has the kind of charm that pulls in women like gravity itself, let’s just say being the wingman was an amazing position to be in. He could only entertain one (maybe two), so there were always opportunities for me, the self-proclaimed "scrub hangin’ out the passenger side."
  

Zero regrets. Those nights were unforgettable.
Westbound Cannonball: From Dulles to Topeka
When orders came through for the Baton Rouge to head west for decommissioning, I turned it into the ultimate solo road trip.
The first stop was obvious: Dave’s house. One last hug for Barbara. One last weekend of Rehoboth flashbacks and basement band plans. Then, in the quiet of the evening, I loaded up the Geo Storm GSI, queued up my best mix tapes, and pointed west.
From Dulles to Topeka, I drove nonstop—36 hours straight. No stimulants beyond what was legally available in gas stations: Vivarin tablets, ephedrine, and the raw inertia of youth and momentum. It was my own personal Cannonball Run, and I was hell-bent on making time.
Looking back, I don’t know if I was running toward something or just refusing to slow down. Probably both. But I do know this: the first leg of that trip, powered by nostalgia, caffeine, and the ghost of the weekend I’d just had with Dave, felt like the purest kind of freedom.
Chapter 25: Cross-Country Freedom and Salt-Soaked Speed
Topeka: Temptation and a Gentle Letdown
Topeka was where the road started to feel like a real sailor’s journey—wild, indulgent, and just a little reckless. Late that night, I did what only sailors seem to do: I found a gentleman's club. One hundred bucks and a handful of bold tips later, I found myself invited back to a hotel room by the dancer I’d been eyeing. I paid for the room, the anticipation was thick… and then, she fell asleep. Dead to the world. Totally unrequited. Honestly, it was probably for the best.
Boulder: Ghosts of Girls and Familiar Malls
From there, I set my sights on Boulder, Colorado, where Precious—yes, that was really her name—a girl I had dated briefly during one of my visits with Dave, was enrolled. Despite my best efforts and a full day spent exploring the town and a shopping center that felt exactly like Lake Forest Mall back in Gaithersburg, Maryland, we never did reconnect. No cell phones. No DMs. Just hopes—and a lot of unreturned calls to dorm landlines. Still, I carry a fond memory of that day, the unfamiliar streets, and the warmth of possibility that never quite materialized.
Utah Awakening: Beauty in the Quiet Hours
Crossing into Utah, I remember pulling over to sleep on an on-ramp sometime in the early morning hours. When I woke up, it was to one of the most breathtaking sunrises I had ever seen—vast, open desert terrain painted in hues I wasn’t used to growing up on the East Coast. The light felt clean. Honest. Like I had driven off the edge of the map and into a better version of reality.
Salt Fever: Bonneville at 124 MPH
Then came the Bonneville Salt Flats—a pilgrimage site for anyone even remotely obsessed with speed records. As a kid, I had devoured every story about the Blue Flame, the Budweiser Rocket Car, and the legends who raced across this otherworldly surface. The access road stretched into the white abyss, and I couldn't help myself. I pinned the pedal of my Geo Storm GSI to the floor, redlined all the way, chasing dreams and trying to punch through the sky. I hit 124 mph, drag-limited, but triumphant nonetheless. I stopped short of the actual lakebed—the salt was soft and wet—but the moment? Pure velocity. Pure joy.
Tahoe, Serendipity, and the $5 Tortellini
Not long after, I saw a sign for Lake Tahoe, and something just pulled me toward it. No GPS back then—just my tattered Rand McNally Atlas with scribbles and coffee stains guiding me west. Around 9:00 PM on a Friday night, I was winding along a two-lane road when I saw two attractive girls walking in the moonlight.
Naturally, I stopped. They said they were headed to a bonfire and asked if I wanted to join. Did I ever.
We pulled up to a clearing on a hill lit by fire and filled with locals—maybe a hundred people, laughter echoing under a dusty, moonlit sky. It felt timeless. When the bonfire wrapped, they looked at me and said, "The best meal in this part of the country? A casino in Reno."
So we went. They weren’t lying—tortellini alla fini with mushroom sauce, absolutely drowning in creamy perfection, for $5.00. Even then, that was a steal. I treated both of them, shared a few stories, and eventually dropped them off. No numbers exchanged, no promises made. Just a night of dust, firelight, and pasta.
And then, back to the road. Still headed west. Still smiling.
Chapter 26: Vallejo, Angles and Dangles, and Full Steam Ahead
First Impressions of Vallejo and a Hidden Geo Storm
From Reno, I raced down through central California, tasting a brand-new kind of freedom. Everything felt different. The humidity—or lack thereof—was a revelation. Your sweat actually worked. It evaporated, leaving you cool and comfortable instead of sticky and miserable. For a guy raised in the swampy air of the East Coast, this was nothing short of revolutionary.
I arrived in Vallejo, and it was like stepping into another planet—my first Jack in the Box and a completely foreign landscape of WWII-era bunkers, dry heat, and gritty industrial charm. I parked my beloved Geo Storm GSI in one of the old concrete naval bunkers along the Mare Island shoreline, where it would sit until I flew back to Norfolk for the boat transit. It felt like I was stashing a part of myself for safekeeping.
Family on Board: Angles and Dangles
While I was making my way west, the USS Baton Rouge held a dependents' cruise, a special event where family members are invited aboard for a taste of submarine life. Both my father and brother joined, and it was one of those rare full-circle moments—family stepping into the world I had immersed myself in.
They were awestruck by the "Angles and Dangles" exercise, where the boat performs dramatic maneuvers, including an emergency blow that sends the sub rocketing upward at steep inclines. For once, they didn’t just hear about it—they felt it. It gave them a visceral appreciation of what submarine service really meant, and gave me pride in showing them what I did for a living.
Final Goodbyes and Full Flank to Mare Island
Back in Norfolk, it was time to wrap things up. I packed the rest of my meager belongings, said goodbye to my few remaining connections, and reported for the boat’s final cruise. This wasn’t your standard crawl-speed transit either. We were hauling ass.
Because the Baton Rouge still had significant reactor life left, we didn’t need to baby the core like most submarines on their final legs. We were flank speed through the Atlantic, Gulf, and Pacific—and you could feel it. Most times, subs glide silently, so smooth you barely remember you're underway. Not this time. The rhythmic oscillation of the hull, the steady hum of speed slicing through the sea—you were reminded constantly that you were aboard a war machine with somewhere to be.

Victoria, British Columbia: A Week of Solace
One of the hidden gifts of the journey came when we stopped in Victoria, BC for what turned into an unexpected week of liberty. Still tinged with the sting of our cancelled Amsterdam port call the previous year, this stop brought needed balance.
Victoria was a gorgeous fusion of British influence and Pacific Coast chill. We wandered cobblestone-like alleys, sampled the local ales, mingled with friendly Canadians, and—most importantly—reconnected as a crew. You could argue that port calls were the Navy’s best team-building exercise, and Victoria proved that tenfold.
Sailors Forged in Steel
As we crept closer to Mare Island, the Baton Rouge began its final approach. There was an odd electricity in the air—equal parts relief, reflection, and anticipation. For many of us, this was the end of an era.
There were still drills. Still long hours. Still the constant readiness that submarine life demanded. But there was also a deepening camaraderie—a bond formed in steel, pressure, and shared hardship.
This journey wasn’t just about a vessel being decommissioned. It was about a crew navigating their own transitions—young men becoming leaders, shipmates becoming brothers, and lives being shaped one dive, one watch, one TDU tube at a time.
Deactivation: Less Systems, More Freedom
As the deactivation of the USS Baton Rouge progressed, a fascinating and fortuitous pattern began to emerge. Each system we shut down translated to one less maintenance burden—a direct and tangible improvement to our daily lives.
This shift in pace wasn’t just technical—it was liberating. We had fewer drills. Less midnight oil to burn. The ship began to quiet. As each pump, valve, or generator was tagged out and powered down, we inched closer to a sailor’s dream: liberty.
The Spare Parts Nightmare
Before all that, though, we lived through what I now consider one of the most galling inefficiencies of submarine life: the Navy spare parts system.
While we were still stationed in Norfolk, another sub in Submarine Squadron 8 urgently needed a 10K distiller lower-pressure brine pump. As fate would have it, that pump was identical in every way—form, fit, function—to our auxiliary seawater pumps. Same manufacturer. Same model. Same size. Same mounts. Identical. Except, of course, for one minor and profoundly Navy-specific detail: the part number was different.
And so, the Navy being the Navy, there was no chance of just swapping out the easily accessible seawater pump. Instead, we had to fully tag out the 10K distiller system, drain and isolate it, and disassemble a maze of piping and cabling to surgically extract the pump from deep inside the unit. Only then could we hand the pump over to the sub that was preparing to get underway.
After that, we waited on a new pump, reversed the whole process, and spent days putting the system back together again.
This kind of senseless logistical red tape was how our “non-duty” days routinely stretched from 0730 to 1930, and duty days turned into 20-hour grinds. It was just one of the many realities that made life in Machinist’s Mate division an often brutal exercise in perseverance.
Shutdown Schedules and Four-Section Liberty
But as we got further into the deactivation process, things started to shift.
With each system that went offline, our lives got a little lighter. Our days now began with an 0830 muster, followed by a few hours of light training or task work. By 1130, we were typically released—liberty granted for the rest of the day.
With the ship no longer in an operational cycle, we adopted a four-section duty rotation. This meant every fourth night, I would stand Shutdown Rover Watch—patrolling the now-defueled and dormant submarine during the silent overnight hours.
Rover Watch: Alone on a War Machine
Those nights left a deep impression on me. There was something hauntingly beautiful about walking the full length of the Baton Rouge alone. With every echo of my boots down the passageways, I was reminded of what the ship once was—a billion-dollar nuclear-powered war machine, now resting in slumber.
It was eerie. And solemn. And yet… I loved it.
There was a profound sense of pride in being the only living soul aboard, entrusted with guarding this technological leviathan while the world outside went on sleeping.
Perspective from the Dry Dock
Whether standing rover, glancing up at the hull from dry dock, or walking down the gangway into a Caribbean port, I always carried a heavy but welcome pride.
To emerge from that stealthy hull into the light, seeing the stares of onlookers—some filled with curiosity, others with awe—made it clear: this wasn’t just a job. This was a calling, even if it meant oozing TDUs, endless brightwork, or archaic parts catalogs.
Every painful maintenance task. Every missed port call. Every long day. It was part of something bigger. And even in the quiet days of shutdown, you still felt it.
Chapter 27: The Horse and Cow, Part I — Relics, Revelry, and the Woman Who Changed My Life
A Bar Steeped in Submarine Lore
It was shortly after arriving at Mare Island that I met the woman who would eventually become my wife, Anita. The scene of this pivotal moment? A place that could only exist in a town like Vallejo—a town with salt in its bones and submarines in its soul. That place was the Horse and Cow.
More than just a bar, the Horse and Cow was a cathedral to submarine culture. Walk through its doors and you weren’t stepping into a dive—you were stepping into a museum. Every inch of wall space, ceiling tile, and support beam was plastered with naval memorabilia: ship plaques, belt buckles, toilet seats from decommissioned boats, photos from tours long past, challenge coins, and even full-sized rigging pieces. It was all there, honoring the legacy of submariners past and present, especially those connected to the Mare Island Naval Shipyard.
The Float and the Flavor
The centerpiece—both absurd and majestic—was a roughly 40-foot-long parade float modeled after a submarine, resting casually next to the dance floor like a sleeping giant. The bar thrummed with a soundtrack that jumped between country hits, '90s rock, and the occasional synth-beat club track. In one corner, pool balls cracked with mechanical regularity. In another, barstools creaked as lifers, lovers, and lost souls nursed cheap drinks and swapped sea stories.
It had a scent—beer, bleach, brass polish, and nostalgia—and that scent is burned into memory.
Meeting Anita
It was on one of those random, nothing-special nights that fate flipped a coin.
I was out with my roommate Bud Thompson, a guy who'd been my ride-or-die through many a hazy evening. We bellied up to the bar like we had a hundred times before. And there she was—Anita, working the room like she owned it without even trying. She wasn’t flashy; she was magnetic. Older than me, confident, stylish, and with a look in her eye like she’d seen a few things but hadn’t let the world break her. We locked eyes, shared a laugh, and before the night was over, I knew I’d met someone different.
We talked. We danced. We shared stories. And somewhere between the jukebox and that parade float, something real began.
The Night That Changed It All
The Horse and Cow wasn’t just the place I met Anita—it was also the scene of a cautionary tale wrapped in liquor and blurred intentions. One night, after more drinks than I should’ve had, I got caught up in a bizarre situation involving a girl and her friend—one of those experiences you laugh about later, but also one that slaps a little truth into you.
It was that night I made a decision: no more drinking. Not for a while, anyway. The clarity that came the morning after changed the trajectory of my life. It was a silly little misfire in the moment, but it planted a seed—that maybe it was time to grow up a bit.
Funny how those moments work. You're not planning them. You’re not looking for them. But they show up, drunk and dressed in irony, and they shape your future.
The Horse and Cow? Yeah, it was just a bar. But it was also the first domino in a line of profound personal milestones—a bar that served more than just drinks. It served turning points.
Chapter 28: The Horse and Cow, Part II — The Fight Before the Farewell
The Missed Shot and the One That Landed
Before I dive into the early days of my courtship with Anita, I’ve got to recount one of the more momentous and unexpected fights of my life, and it happened right where so many stories of my Navy years found their stage: the Horse and Cow.
I was there, as I often was in those final days before leaving Mare Island, with one simple goal: muster the courage to ask out Yoli, the beautiful, sharp-witted bartender who’d caught my eye from day one. She was older, confident, always composed—and in another one of life’s predictable ironies, when I finally worked up the nerve to ask her out, she said yes.
But I was heading out. My time at Mare Island was nearing its end, and with it, that door closed too. Just another missed opportunity to stack onto the long list we all carry—the ones that got away.
The Accusation
That night, I wasn’t drinking. I had given up alcohol earlier that year—a personal choice born from too many nights where bad decisions came free with every third beer. I was sipping ice water behind the bar, playing pool, listening to music, and feeling the strange weight of imminent transition.
Three sailors were next to me, doing what sailors do: playing pool, drinking, running their mouths. One of them gave me a strange look. “Did you dye your hair before?” he asked. “Didn’t you used to be heavier?” I laughed it off. I’d lost a lot of weight, dyed my hair black for a stretch, and yeah—I looked different. “That’s me,” I replied. “Put in the work, dropped some pounds.”
Then came the punchline—or the gut-punch.
“You were hitting on my wife.”
The Fight
His tone wasn’t playful, and now the tension was thick. I looked at him, somewhere between confused and disbelieving. I tried to deescalate, saying, “C’mon man, no harm, no foul. Just talking. It’s all fun and games.”
What I should’ve said was something like: “You brought your wife to the Horse and Cow? Seriously? What’d you expect?” But I didn’t. I kept it cool. Or tried to.
The slap he aimed at me was weak—a flailing move, not a punch. Instinct took over. I shot in and grappled him to the floor. Years of high school wrestling and wax-on-wax-off dojo drills kicked in. I had him grounded, and started landing some blows, but I’d overlooked one thing: his two shipmates.
Suddenly, boots were slamming into my side and back, the two of them piling on while I was still entangled with their buddy. I managed to twist out, got to my feet, and limped off with just a bruised ankle—but it was a fight I hadn’t asked for, and one I wasn’t going to back down from.
The Aftermath
The next morning, I passed out in the shower.
The pain from the ankle swelled into something deeper—a dull, all-consuming ache—and I collapsed, naked and alone, in the stall. I came to, soaked and stunned, and hobbled back to my room before heading to medical.
Of course, medical didn’t see a three-on-one assault. They saw a sailor in a fight. I got a reprimand—one that would shadow me later, when newly frocked Ken Whitson decided to make an example of me for what was, by any sane standard, a minor infraction.
But I never tried to lie or make excuses. I didn’t throw the first punch—or the first slap, for that matter. I stood my ground. And sometimes? Sometimes you just have to fight.
Chapter 28: The Horse and Cow, Part III — The Girl, the Roommate, and the Dart
The Encounter
As it happened, the story of how I met Anita, my future wife, is... well, a little embarrassing. But it’s part of the tapestry, and to leave it out would be dishonest.
It was a typical night at the Horse and Cow, that shrine to submariners and ill-advised choices. My roommate Bud Thompson and I had already logged many hours there, soaking in the atmosphere, the history, and the cheap drinks. That night, Anita stood out. She was with a townie—nothing special about him—but she? She was elfin, tiny at just 4'11", and strikingly beautiful in a way that was both delicate and magnetic.
I was too shy to say a word. Bud wasn’t. He went for it, struck up a conversation, and came back with her number.
Then he disappeared.
For a week.
When he returned to our room, he looked defeated—holding a container of home-cooked food Anita had made for him. “That girl is crazy,” he said, “I've known her a week and she's already cooking for me and telling me she loves me!” He was done.
I thought, That sounds great, and without hesitation, I asked for her number.
January 15th, 1994
Our first date was my birthday—January 15th. We hit some rock bar, tagging along with her friend Tirena, who was infatuated with a guy in the band. I don’t remember the music, but I remember the way everything clicked. Later that night, after dropping off Tirena, Anita and I ended up back at her apartment in Vallejo.
Cue the universal law of unintended consequences:
“Be careful what you wish for—you might just get it.”
The Dodge Dart and the Invisible Husband
Anita lived right off base. The apartment complex had a pool, hot tub, and a seriously nostalgic Dodge Dart parked outside. I found it charming—until I found out it belonged to her current husband.
The backstory was straight out of a military soap opera. Her husband, a former A-Ganger aboard the Kamiah Maiah (a converted special ops boomer), had taken geo-bachelor orders to Hawaii. Anita stayed behind. Her excuse? She “needed to be close for her grandmother’s funeral.” The kicker? Both grandmothers were still alive, well into their 80s.
That should’ve been a red flag. But love—and lust—makes fools of us all.
Boot Camp Proposals and Other Bad Ideas
To be fair, her husband didn’t have a prayer. He met her through a mutual friend during their first boot camp leave in Chillicothe, and within a week, he proposed. Because, why not? You meet a girl on leave, you're 18 and infatuated—it seemed like the thing to do.
I've done a lot of reflection since then. I don’t say any of this to mock him. He was probably a decent guy—just in way over his head. I have since tried to live by an ethos of minimizing harm. That relationship, from where I stood, was already over, even if the paperwork said otherwise.
I wish him peace. I really do.
Bud, The Fallout, and the Healing
As for Bud—our friendship didn't survive unscathed. I was smug, I won’t lie. I didn’t hide my happiness with Anita. In fact, I rubbed it in. While he stewed in the aftermath of their brief crash-and-burn, I basked in my newfound relationship. I got cocky. And I know it hurt him.
Years later, just before I went in, Bud and I reconnected. We had a long, cathartic talk. He apologized for what had happened in the shipyard. I apologized too—for the things I said and did that rubbed salt in those wounds. That conversation brought me a lot of peace.
I’d carried some of those wounds for a long time. He had too. Funny how two guys in their early twenties, caught in the throes of rivalry, booze, and heartbreak, could do that much damage. But healing’s real, and I’m grateful we had the chance to set things right.
Chapter 29: Exit Strategy — Anita, Skates, and the Shape of Things
Pilot Wings, Vallejo Eats, and the Honeymoon Phase
In the beginning, my time with Anita was genuinely enjoyable. We’d hit restaurants around Vallejo, but mostly, we did what sailors do best—practice. In the quiet hours, we’d log countless missions on Pilot Wings 64, burning hours on her Nintendo 64 while the rest of the world faded into background noise. For a while, it felt like a groove. A rhythm.
The Break
But rhythms fade. Over time, my growing unease with the relationship started to surface. I had the Navy, and the base, and the daily grind of duty to hide behind. That shield gave me enough distance to break it off. She kept tabs, sure—through her friendships with my shipmates—but I was able to cut ties cleanly. No hard feelings. No explosions. Just a silent fade to black.
Several months later, she moved to San Diego, where her husband, still active-duty, got a permanent change of duty station. One weekend, I made the drive down to see her. We met at the Denny’s across the street from where she was staying. And when I saw her—it was like that scene from The Mask:
"Our love is like a red, red rose, and I am a little thorny."
Before me stood a miniature Daisy Duke, dimensionally flawless, radiating like a walking punchline to all my best efforts at discipline. What was I to do?
Body Image and the War Within
At the same time, I was waging a completely different war—a private one.
I’ve said it before: I’ve battled weight my entire life. But during our ship’s stay at Mare Island Naval Shipyard, I crossed a threshold. I developed an anorexia-adjacent obsession with weight loss. I was working out four to six hours a day and eating next to nothing.
From my peak of 240 pounds at the beginning of drydock, I dropped to 165 pounds by the time I exited the Navy in September 1994.
This wasn’t just about looks. This was about control. About fighting for mastery over something in my life. And weirdly enough, it came with some benefits.
Raves, Ice Rinks, and 5 AM Muster
I dedicated Sundays and Mondays to skating—no matter what. I became a regular at the Berkeley Ice Arena, particularly for the adult sessions. That’s where I met a girl who taught lessons, and we became fast platonic friends. She introduced me to a scene I hadn’t seen before—house parties, late-night raves, and underground clubs.
Thanks to the odd schedules at Mare Island, I could make it work. We'd be out until 5:00 AM, I’d power through muster, then crash for four or five hours before getting up and doing it all over again. Skating, raving, and starving—an unlikely but strangely liberating combo that defined the final months of my Navy career.
Chapter 30: Skating Valleys, Losing Grip
The Contra Costa Canal Trail — Pure Freedom on Wheels
During my final year in the Navy, one of the most grounding experiences was discovering the Contra Costa Canal Trail network. It stretched for hundreds of miles, winding through valleys, beside concrete canals, under sun-soaked skies. The smooth pavement and scenic views made it a skater’s paradise. I spent countless hours on my inline skates, carving through suburban beauty and pastoral stillness. It was meditation in motion.
Diverse Company, Clear Identity
Around this time, I built a pretty eclectic friend group. While there wasn’t much ethnic diversity, there was a broad spectrum of sexual identities. For clarity’s sake—and with no offense meant—I’ll say again: I’m 100% heterosexual. I’ve always been wired to love the female form. Still, I found these friendships fulfilling, and I admired the authenticity many of them had in living out who they were.
The Girl from Novato — Almost Something More
In this whirlwind of transformation, I met a girl who stood out. Her family was fabulously wealthy, living on a mountaintop in Novato. It felt like something out of a John Hughes script—rich girl, Navy guy, different worlds colliding. I messed it up, of course. But even after we drifted apart, we reconnected years later when she became a cruise director for Norwegian Cruise Lines. We shared a week-long tryst. Like many chapters in my love life, it ended with no hard feelings, but no lasting connection either. Still—what an adventure.
Victim Stance and the Chip on My Shoulder
The truth is, I wasn’t in a great headspace. RMA had instilled in me a kind of conceit, a sense that I was better than the life I found as an enlisted man. I carried that chip into every interaction. On the surface, I was respectful—"yes sir," "no sir"—but my tone was brittle. My attitude gave me away.
If I were to do it all over again, I think they’d feel the difference. Real respect. True humility. But back then, I was angry and insecure, constantly feeling my stomach to check whether the fat had returned. I had this weird tic—smelling my wrist—something that probably came off even stranger than it was.
Bud and the Norfolk Reunion — The Dark Turn
Things got worse at home. Bud, my once-friend and roommate, turned on me. He’d teamed up with my former Norfolk roommate—his new best friend—and together they made it their mission to break me. One night, they destroyed the bicycle assigned to me by the shipyard, leaving it twisted in front of the barge like some middle school mafia hit. It wasn’t subtle. It was cruel, and it broke something inside me.
Midwatch and the Breaking Point
In my watch section, E5 James Woods, an Engineering Laboratory Technician Machinist's Mate, kept showing up late to relieve me. Every single duty day, the guy would sleep in later and later while I covered the gap. There was an unspoken rule: Don’t rat. Don’t tell. He leaned on that, hard.
Then, one day, he just didn’t show up at all. Slept through an entire midwatch.
Me? I said nothing. But I was boiling over.
Chapter 31: The Beginning of the End
The Retaliation That Backfired
The next duty day, fed up and quietly seething, I made a conscious decision to be ten minutes late relieving ELT James Woods. I figured it was fair—he’d been late constantly, and I’d been the good soldier every time. But he wasn’t one to let it go. He reported me to his section chief, which wasn’t even in my chain of command. That complaint triggered a confrontation between us, an argument loud enough to be overheard by our newly frocked-to-Chief but still technically an E6, Kenneth Whitson.
Meet the "Diggit"
Whitson was a classic Navy archetype—a “Diggit.” The guy ate, breathed, and slept the Navy, and we resented him for it. He always showed up early. He never left on time. He’d post up in the barge office with his feet on a desk, whistling like a lunatic and accomplishing nothing, 12 hours a day. While the rest of us counted the days, he made the Navy his entire identity.
But now he had a new stripe—a Chief’s anchor freshly frocked to his collar. He was eager to prove something, and unfortunately, I was the one who gave him an excuse.
The Missed Meeting
After overhearing the argument, Whitson told both Woods and me to meet him on the barge after muster the next morning. But the following morning, I was still in my routine. After a duty day, you typically got to hit the trail after morning muster. That’s what I did—completely forgetting the meeting. It wasn’t defiance. It was just muscle memory.
When I came back aboard later, everyone knew. The whispers were all around the barge—Whitson was on a warpath. I heard that he’d filed charges against me.
Article 92 — Wait, What?
Charges? That word hit me like a torpedo.
Shortly after, it was made official: Article 92 — Failure to Follow a Lawful Order. I was stunned. No counsel. No briefing. No warning. Just paperwork and procedure. Suddenly I was facing serious military punishment for what felt like a non-event.
Here I was—a guy who had kept his mouth shut through months of hazing, missed watches, and outright sabotage. I had taken it all on the chin, trying to finish my time with as much honor and dignity as I could muster. And now, this?
I was confused, angry, and—more than anything—I felt betrayed.
Chapter 32: Captain’s Mast and the Fallout
The Decision to Face the Music
So—ignorantly, naively—I chose to go to Captain's Mast.
I thought, Hey, I spent all that time with Commander Kolbeck on the ORSE inspection team. He knows me. He knows my work ethic. Surely, he’ll hear me out. What’s the worst that could happen?
I pressed my uniform to perfection, marched into his office on the barge, and sat down across from the man who once greeted me with familiarity and respect. I told him my side of the story—calm, composed, honest. And when I finished?
He demoted me.
Just like that. No sympathy. No discussion. Just a drop to E3 and a vague punishment I don’t even clearly remember anymore—probably some extra duty or restriction, but the sting of the rank loss drowned it all out.
The Real Cost of a Stripe
Losing E4 wasn’t just a matter of pride—though that was a massive part of it—it was also a gut punch to my finances. The demotion cost me:
* $100/month in Sea Pay
* $100/month in Sub Pay
* Another $200/month from the E4-E3 base pay difference
That’s $400/month gone, instantly. But more than that, it was the humiliation. I had to have all my uniforms re-sewn. Everyone knew. It was a public walk of shame in the tight-knit world of submariners.
And looming on the horizon? I was slated to be transferred to another 688-class sub, the USS Asheville. That idea haunted me—I was convinced it would break me for good. Luckily—or perhaps fatefully—I would never set foot on that boat.
Breaking Point
The demotion wrecked me. I remember sitting alone, feeling utterly dismantled. I had put so much into my Navy career—graduated in the top of my class, earned respect in my division, trained relentlessly, showed up early, stayed late.
And now? I was backstabbed by a guy who couldn’t even wake up on time... sabotaged by two bitter roommates… and finally, betrayed by a commander I believed would do the right thing.
I wanted to die. For real.
I stood in the corner of that dusty barge room, surrounded by power plant manuals, operating instructions, engineering logs… and somehow I found the Navy personnel manual tucked among them. In that thick tome was my first glimpse of hope. A small paragraph. A possibility:
"Service members facing a significant loss of rate or unsuitable assignment may apply for early separation under honorable conditions.”
That was it.
I loved the Navy—that’s the truth. But I could not continue as an E3, humiliated, ignored, and shoved into an unforgiving pipeline. I had just over two years left on my contract. And the way I saw it then—those two years would be worse than prison.
And now, with 8.5 years of real incarceration behind me, I can confirm it absolutely would have been.
A New Path Forward
Armed with the info, I went straight to the ship's newly assigned senior corpsman, our Doc. I told him flat out:
“Doc, I need to see the shrink in Oakland.”
Maybe it was my tone. Maybe it was the honesty in my eyes. Or maybe he just knew the look of a man standing at the edge. He didn’t fight me. He just nodded.
Chapter 33: Oakland, the Shrink, and the Hat Trick
The Ride to Oakland
So I hopped in the trusty red Geo Storm GSI—still bearing road grime from a dozen other cross-country stunts—and headed down to Oakland. No GPS, just intuition, a glovebox copy of the Rand McNally, and whatever rage was left to burn.
The appointment was with a senior Navy shrink, uniformed and seated behind a desk that probably hadn’t moved since Vietnam. I didn’t bother with ceremony.
I looked him dead in the eye and said:
“If you don’t let me out, I’m going to find the steepest hill I can and skate down it until I break something. Then I’m going to do it again. And again.”
He studied me for a second—leaned back a little, arms folded like he’d heard this before. Then came the reply:
“You don’t have to go through all that. I can have you out in two weeks.”
Just like that.
The Last Laugh
I drove back to the barge with the biggest grin I’d worn in months. Not out of malice—just pure, unfiltered relief. The kind that settles in your chest when you realize you aren’t crazy after all. You were just in a crazy situation.
I walked into the lounge and told everyone:
“I’m out in two weeks. Honorable. That’s right.”
To a man, no one believed me. Not a single sailor had heard of it happening. One even asked if I forged papers. I didn’t. It was real.
I pulled a hat trick—walked out of the Navy clean, with an honorable discharge, and no more Chief Whitson breathing down my neck or ELT Woods gaslighting me into a disciplinary spiral. I was free.
No Counseling, No Map
Here’s the thing though—no one gave me a transition plan. The command was stretched thin. The shipyard was a skeleton crew. And no one sat me down to explain the VA system, or that I’d be eligible for disability.
They handed me my DD-214 and waved goodbye. No exit interview. No medical review board. No acknowledgment that I’d spent the last year with a crushed ego, an eating disorder, and a spiral of sabotage.
It would be twenty years later, after prison, after the wreckage, that I’d finally walk into a VA office and be told:
“You qualify for 70% disability.”
Seventy percent.
Twenty years too late—but not too late to mean something.

Chapter 34: The Integra and the Cost of Speed
A Bad Trade at the Worst Time
One of the biggest gut punches from that demotion wasn’t just the humiliation or the financial loss from rank and pay. It was the crushing reality that I’d just traded in my 1990 Geo Storm GSI—the same car that carried me cross-country, up mountains, past salt flats and through every major checkpoint of my Navy career—for a brand new Acura Integra GSR sedan.
It was pristine. Technological perfection. It was the car equivalent of an F-22 Raptor dressed up as a family sedan. I was in love.
And now, I couldn’t afford it.
Even though I’d inherited around $160,000 from my grandparents, the trust had rules. It was a protective structure—not something I could just pull from casually. I guess if I had really needed help, it could’ve been arranged. But I wasn’t thinking in terms of backup plans. I was thinking in terms of dignity, of self-sufficiency, of keeping my head above water.
Speed Was the Entertainment
Another lesson I learned from that time, one that stuck with me throughout life, was the true reason I drove.
It wasn’t transportation. It was entertainment.
Everything I needed back then—food, clothing, shelter, even camaraderie—was covered by the Navy. The car? That was my escape, my joy, my arena of competition. I wasn’t content to just get from A to B. I needed to break my own records every time.
Whether it was driving from Vallejo to Berkeley, or making a run down to the rink in Orlando, I was always shaving minutes, beating clock ghosts that no one else could see.
Pulled Over in Orlando
There was one night in particular I remember: driving my Geo Storm GSI to the ice-skating rink in Orlando, focused on speed and music. Out of nowhere—blue lights. I had no idea why.
The officer approached. Cool, not hostile. He didn’t ask why I was in a hurry. He didn’t ask if I was military. And I didn’t volunteer it.
He disappeared with my license and papers. When he returned, he didn’t scold me. He gave me a life lesson wrapped in Navy respect:
“Here you go, Petty Officer Courtney. Please remember to signal when you change lanes.”
No ticket. Just a message.
From that day forward, I’ve signaled every lane change. Even when I’m the only car on the road. Even when no one’s watching.
Because sometimes, you don’t signal for others—you signal for yourself, or for God, or just to remind the universe that you’re paying attention.
  

It’s in the Getting There
At some point, it finally hit me—why the hell was I speeding everywhere?
Every drive had become a race. But what was I racing toward? A red light? A line at the drive-thru? A bed I’d already slept in?
That’s when I started to think about that cross-country road trip from Norfolk to Mare Island—the one where I got lost, got found, picked up hitchhikers, hit 124 mph on the Bonneville Salt Flats, and met two strangers who took me to a Reno buffet after a bonfire in the hills.
That was the destination. And it wasn’t about speed—it was about the ride.
When I’m in my car, I’m in the most comfortable seat I own. Everything I need is right there:
* Too hot? Turn down the AC.
* Tired? Fire up some techno on the quadraphonic 1000-watt custom stereo system.
* Need sleep? Pull over at a rest stop, recline the seat, and let the stars keep watch.
That’s when it clicked—there’s no place I’d rather be than behind the wheel of a well-equipped, mid-level (or better) American car. And once you realize that, why rush?
Why burn past the moment that is the point? Life doesn’t always happen at the destination—it’s in the winding asphalt between here and there. And every click of the odometer is a story waiting to be told.

Autobiography: Book 2
Chapter 1: Exiting the Navy: Rudderless
After exiting the military in October of 1994, I took a long, slow ride back from California, meandering through Arkansas, Maryland, Norfolk, Radford, and West Virginia. Somewhere along that journey, I saw my grandfather—possibly for the last time.
That first trip as a civilian, driving cross-country in my 1994 Acura Integra GSR, was when I truly embraced what would become my life’s unofficial motto: Hakuna Matata. I hadn’t heard the phrase yet—the Lion King wouldn’t hit my radar until later—but the philosophy was already embedded in me: it’s in the getting there that counts.
I remembered all those solo boat rides on Lake Tarpon in my 1996 Sea-Doo Challenger. I’d pilot out to the center of the lake, kill the engine, lie back, and just stare up at the sky. Alone. At peace. But in doing that often enough, I also realized something: boating is boring. Unless you have a destination and people to share the ride with, it’s just floating with a price tag. I eventually sold the boat—clocking 186 hours on the engine—and later, the house too. Ugh.
For this cross-country return to Florida, I took the Southern route. From Vallejo, I dropped south to Bakersfield and then picked up Route 40 eastward. I stopped at the Grand Canyon, but somehow missed Las Vegas entirely. At the time, I had no idea what a big deal Vegas already was as a cultural icon and tourist magnet. What I did find, though, was a meditative rhythm. I drove long legs at exact speed limits with cruise control engaged, squeezing every mile out of each gallon. It was the first time in my life I saw efficiency as a form of peace.
The Acid, the Carnival, and the Roaches
My first big stop was Arkansas, where I visited my old friend Dave. Since our last hangout in Northern Virginia, Dave had toured with Phish and eventually settled down in Arkansas with Gina, one of his high school friends. They were in college—maybe in Arkadelphia. I’m not sure I ever confirmed that.
I rolled off I-40 and was greeted by imposing signs listing Arkansas’ severe gun laws—something I wasn’t used to seeing. I arrived in the afternoon, and true to Dave’s spirit, his idea for the night was this: drop some acid and hit the local town fair. That was the kind of surreal, jarring, sensory-overloaded experience only LSD can fully deliver. I hadn’t touched the stuff in at least two years—since my California days partying with Ken, Andy, and Lou—and maybe four years since doing anything that wild.
Dave had been living in that trailer for over a year. Just before my visit, his neighbor had shot and killed his dog, and put a bullet through the window of his car. It was a grim, rednecky mess. The trailer was roach-infested, and their workaround was to sleep in tents inside their bedrooms to avoid them. Not exactly my idea of hospitality.
That night, trying to sleep in the “guest room,” I felt a creeping sensation. When I turned on the light, I saw hundreds of cockroaches around me. That was it. I was done. I said my goodbyes, packed up, and left. I love Dave like a brother, but even brothers have boundaries. That trip, bizarre carnival, acid-laced night, and the roachpocalypse... that was enough.
Radford, Kelly, and Family Fractures
Next stop was Radford University in Virginia to visit my cousin Kelly Courtney. She gave me directions to her off-campus housing, and I rolled in for what would be the last time I’d ever see her.
Kelly and her three roommates—all stunning, by the way—welcomed me warmly. We had a great, brief reunion. But afterward, Kelly vanished from all sides of the family—particularly our side. She was the daughter of my uncle Steve, my father’s youngest brother, who had been largely estranged from us. Kelly felt abandoned by her dad, especially because he didn’t pay child support. Her mother, who had remarried into a more financially stable household, gave her a better material life, but the rift with Steve never healed.
She was my favorite cousin growing up. Steve had two other daughters—Heather and Holly—with Aunt Catherine, and they were lovely, too. I flew up for both of their weddings on my own dime, only to never hear from them again. That hurt. Especially when I needed them most.
After I got into trouble years later, I wrote Uncle Steve and his family a sincere letter. When it was returned—clearly and coldly marked with a rejection—it stung more than anything. The message was unmistakable: you’re not one of us anymore.
To be fair, even my brother Ron has little to no contact with that side of the family anymore. It’s a shame. Willful ignorance, mostly. But I’ve forgiven them. I just wish they’d have shown up when it mattered.
Greenbelt, Grandfather, and the Ghosts of Race Tracks
I eventually made it to Greenbelt, Maryland, where I saw my grandfather, possibly for the last time. He struggled with hearing loss, but always tried his best to connect with me and Ron. Despite the gaps, I think near the end he was proud of me. I was certainly proud to have him as my grandfather.
He had that old-school hustle. For years, he ran vending machine routes, which gave him flexible hours and time to spend on his true passion: horse racing. I remember those smoke-choked afternoons at Laurel and Bowie racetracks—me, him, and my grandmother, eating packed lunches while he read the racing form like scripture.
Later, he pivoted to flipping Section 8 rental properties. He’d buy, rehab, and rent out these homes for steady income. It was honest work, and he built a solid retirement out of it.
Their home in Greenbelt was filled with the relics of a well-lived life. I’d get lost in the attic and basement, exploring old model planes, a childhood minibike, and mildewy boxes of Americana. The place had a scent—dust, oil, memories—and it has never left my nose or mind.

Chapter 2: Penpower
While effectively rudderless after my exit from the Navy, I developed a fairly sophisticated system for hunting down my next role—ideally, as a power plant operator. Unfortunately, the mid-1990s were marked by reductions in force at many nuclear power plants, making that path a dead end.
As I had done when I discovered RMA in the back pages of Boy’s Life, I now turned to the classifieds at the back of Navy Times, where I found the ad that would define the next chapter of my life: Penpower – Power Plant Startup, Operations, and Support.

Penpower’s founders were all former Navy nuclear-certified, submariner-qualified ELT machinist's mates. They had met at Knolls Atomic Power Laboratory, and while working for GE, discovered they were being significantly underpaid compared to civilian contractors. Chapter 3: I Tought I Saw a Putty Tat! (Circuit City Edition)

I got the idea to apply after spending a lot of downtime browsing the Solano Circuit City while stationed in Vallejo, California. The sales guys always bragged about raking it in. So once I was back in Palm Harbor, I jumped on a chance to get hired for the Christmas season. How bad could it be?

Munny Nat, Flea Clouds, and the Birth of a Bond
Not long before Doug moved out, he brought home a kitten he found in the middle of the street. The thing was tiny—it could fit in the palm of your hand with room to spare. I had a strict no-pets policy at the time, but this little furball was just too cute to resist.

That’s when I saw it: a dense black cloud of fleas leaping off the floor around me—like a fizzy swarm of Sprite bubbles. Thanks, Doug. Not just for ghosting, but for the flea infestation too.
Still, I couldn’t leave the cat.
I bought flea shampoo, collars, and supplies and bathed him in the kitchen sink. As I pulled back the fur, I was horrified to see dozens of fleas casually crawling around his face like they owned the place. Nothing seemed to kill them.
The vet gave me the magic bullet: Advantage. A literal nerve agent for fleas. Within 15 minutes, they were gone. Completely gone. Problem solved.
I named him Munny Nat—short for “Putty Tat,” from the Warner Bros. cartoons. For nearly fifteen years, he was my companion, my shadow, and my meow conversationalist. I’m not exaggerating—we had actual vocal conversations. He’d meow back, matching my tone and timing. Maybe it was mimicry, but maybe not. Either way, we understood each other.
Lessons from a Cat: Affection, Anger, and Regret
Munny Nat taught me something about emotional nuance. When I’d leave for long stretches—weekends in Maryland, extended absences—he let me know he wasn’t happy. Sometimes he’d jump on the bed, curl up... and pee on me. The first time, I was furious. I yelled, swatted him, and stormed out.

During the years Anita and I were separated, I also cared for her cat, Araya, named after the Slayer frontman. When Anita left and didn’t have a place for Araya, I stepped up and made space for both of them in my home.
But when I was arrested, and Anita flew down to retrieve Lauren, she left Munny Nat behind. She didn’t take him. My brother, who was in town at the time, couldn’t step in. And just like that, Munny Nat was left to the system. I’ve had nightmares about it ever since.
He was loyal. He was mine. And I failed him.
Looking back, Circuit City was less a job and more a proving ground. It taught me how to survive in the civilian world: the games, the grift, the value of integrity, and the price of cutting corners. It also gave me a few unexpected gifts—a glimpse at life outside the uniform, a few friendships I’ll never forget, and a tiny, flea-bitten kitten who became my family.

I love you, Munny Nat. Always.

Servers of Middle Earth
Most days I worked remotely, having just moved into a new house. When I did go in, it was to Mike Hunter’s converted two-car garage—a fully climate-controlled server farm humming with ambition.
A dozen Windows 2000 boxes lined the shelving, each named after a Lord of the Rings character: Elrond, Samwise, Bilbo, Arwen, Galadriel, Smaug.
 It was nerd paradise.
Those servers powered our bread and butter: a keyword-jacking, script-based virtual-site generator that gamed search engines like Yahoo, AltaVista, and Ask Jeeves.
At the heart of it was a VB 6 COM+ component—a port of a JavaScript engine I’d written—that did all the heavy lifting.
Each ASP “container” file contained only a few lines of code: it instantiated the COM+ object and let the component generate the page dynamically.
Every time a crawler hit one of those containers, the COM+ service wrote the fully rendered HTML into SQL Server, using the full URL as its primary key.
To multiply coverage, the ASP file replicated itself n times (anywhere from 1 to 10 clones per request), seeding directories until each held tens or even hundreds of thousands of files—so many that Windows Explorer would lock up trying to open the folder.
When a search-engine bot requested a page, it was served directly from SQL; when a human visitor arrived, the code quietly redirected them to the advertiser’s site—the customer paying for the click.
Elegant, audacious, and completely unsustainable.
At peak, we had nearly a billion indexed pages before the hammer came down.

The Crash
For a while, the model worked. We pulled in hundreds of dollars per day in legitimate click-through revenue, with clients like a major air-filter vendor.
We bought dozens of keyword-stuffed domains—aircleaners.com, sunbeamairfilters.net—and let the machine spin armies of landing pages.
Then one morning, every search engine de-indexed us simultaneously.
WebAdTech’s cash printer went silent overnight.
I was let go, but I’d already seen the writing on the wall.
The SEO world had changed in an instant. All the little loopholes vanished.
We’d flown too close to the sun—and got scorched.

It was elegant, fast, and a welcome antidote to the madness of the ad generator.

 A handful of us jumped into Quake 3 Arena deathmatches for 45 minutes.
Cathartic, nerdy, and—for that moment in tech history—completely normal.
Mike, the founder, was a brilliant, restless entrepreneur. His garage incubated several side hustles that could only have existed in 1999:
   * mywetpanties.com – Anita handled about twenty orders herself.

   * myexwife.com – Satirical revenge before “revenge porn” had a name.

   * Gabriel.net – A sprawling adult network grossing $50 K a month until a lawsuit over photo rights imploded it.

It was all par for the pre-bubble era—wild, weird, and gloriously unsustainable.

For me, it was a lesson in impermanence: how fast digital empires can dissolve once the algorithm gods change their minds.
Still, that period forged the instincts I carry into every project since—build clever, but build clean.
 Grey-hat taught me where the edge is. Now I know which side I want to stand on.

Chapter 12: Ceridian — A Million Checks an Hour
The Project That Never Stood a Chance
I joined Ceridian Employer Services in January 2001 for what was supposed to be a short-term contract with a big goal: build a gross-to-net paycheck calculation engine capable of processing one million paychecks per hour. On paper, it sounded like an exciting technical challenge. In practice, it became a case study in how poor leadership can tank even the best-laid plans.
The company had brought in Extreme Logic out of Atlanta to provide their high-volume COM+ message queuing engine—a distributed processing framework to scale the system across an N-tier architecture. This was a big deal at the time—pre-Azure, pre-AWS Lambda—so this was effectively early serverless in spirit, if not in name. The architecture was solid; all we needed to do was implement the endpoints and write the components to handle the queuing logic. In theory, it should have been plug-and-play. In reality… not quite.
Too Many Chiefs, Not Enough Logic
There were two contractors from Extreme Logic, two in-house developers besides me, two business analysts, and a Ceridian project manager trying to herd the cats. But none of it mattered, because the boss—the guy in charge—was a total [bleep]head.
This guy rolled in every day in a Chrysler 300M like it was a chariot of the gods. Divorcing, bitter, arrogant, and clueless. Instead of building on what the prior team had started (they’d already developed a version-one system), he scrapped it completely and decided to start over from scratch. His big idea? “Let’s just write stored procedures for everything to better understand the data.”
Only problem? The data was a mess. There were hundreds of tables, and most of them were half-obscured by business logic layered over years of legacy design decisions. You couldn’t “understand the data” by staring at it in isolation—but try telling that to someone who thinks buzzwords are a substitute for domain expertise.
One Ticket, Hold the Sanity
One moment that still grinds my gears happened midway through the project. I had borrowed a couple of Ceridian's SQL Server and MSDN CDs so I could install them at home and mirror the environment. Completely standard practice for devs back then. I forgot to bring them back one day. This guy makes me leave work in the middle of the day and drive all the way home to get them—and I get nailed for speeding on Dale Mabry Highway coming over the hump.
That moment right there? Peak tech job humiliation. Being forced to drop what you're doing, rush home because of someone else’s lack of trust, and then getting a speeding ticket on top of it. Just one of those slapstick reality checks that tech life loves to hand out from time to time.
Lessons Learned (and Dodged)
We never shipped a million-check engine. The team never really jelled. The boss never got any better. But I walked away with some sharper COM+ chops, a little more SQL Server mileage, and a few battle scars that would serve me well later.
Looking back, Ceridian wasn’t a failure. It was a cautionary tale. The tech was there. The people mostly had the skills. But a project lives or dies by how it’s led—and in this case, it died before it ever had a chance to run.
Chapter 13: Chase Home Finance and the Flow of Things
A $45/hr Paid Vacation—At First
I started at Chase Home Finance in March 2001 as a Senior Programmer Analyst on the “ZiPPY” team—an enterprise group focused on developing and maintaining a massive N-Tier mortgage underwriting platform. The first week? I didn’t even have system access. My boss, who’d hired me, was MIA—constantly out on interviews, probably hunting for his next gig. So I got paid $45 an hour to sit in a cubicle and flip through some old, outdated system manuals.
Once I finally got into the system and PVCS version control, the real work started—and quickly snowballed.
Enter: The Flow Response Generator
One of the tools I was assigned to maintain and improve was called the Flow Response Generator. I fell in love with it. It had an elegantly object-oriented structure that left a lasting impact on my coding approach.
The core idea was genius: files were parsed line-by-line, and each line was stored as a Line object which itself contained a collection of Segment objects. These were structured for easy transformation and output—ideal for credit file processing. But when I got it, it was inefficient as hell. It took over 75 seconds to run because it wasn’t caching its segment collections.
By the time I was done with it, I had it processing in under 300 milliseconds. That kind of performance gain doesn’t just make systems better—it makes developers proud.
A Beautiful, Fragile Architecture
The broader architecture at Chase was a beautiful Rube Goldberg machine. Data flowed in from six different loan origination systems, passed through a directory scanner, then branched to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac connectors, returned with results, and got fed into a credit bureau interface. It was a finely tuned orchestration of high-stakes data movement… with a ticking time bomb embedded inside.
The person I had temporarily replaced—out on pregnancy leave—had commented out about 30 lines of mission-critical logic from the credit check subsystem. Specifically, she’d disabled the part that ensured a credit pull wasn’t redundantly re-issued for the same applicant.
When I found it, it was too late. The system had been hammering credit agencies with unnecessary duplicate pulls. The team racked up over $250,000 in fees—a disaster.
Their response? Cut someone. Guess who?
Yup—the only guy who actually had code in production.
Style Over Substance
Around that time, another team at Chase decided that instead of enhancing the Flow Response Generator, they’d just rebuild the entire thing using a new buzzword stack—COM+ and all the hot middleware jargon of the day.
Sound familiar? It was basically the same story as WebAdTech’s Virtual Site Generator: take something that works, rewrap it in fluff, then pat yourself on the back without ever producing a usable product.
None of those guys ever got code into production. I did. But they all stayed. Every last one. Former baseball players, networkers, code-theorists. Not a single working component between them.
Watching the World Burn
It was also at Chase where I watched the second tower fall. I’ll never forget it. I was on the second floor of One Memorial Center in the breakroom, which overlooked the atrium. There, along with a few coworkers, I watched history unfold live.
The room was silent, except for the faint hum of fluorescent lights and CNN’s coverage echoing from the small wall-mounted TV. It was a surreal, terrifying moment. And somehow, all that chaos felt like a metaphor for corporate tech at the time—beautiful structure, huge ambition, but fragile in ways you’d never expect.
Mike Melberg
I was hired on the same day as Mike Melberg, a guy I immediately respected. He had an electrical engineering degree from either Columbia or University of Chicago, always showed up sharp in pressed khakis, and drove a clean, understated Lexus—one of the good ones, like the 300 series.
Mike and I hit it off instantly. We had lunch together almost every day for the first month. He told me about taking care of his ex-wife and their son, how he’d made sure they had counseling, support, everything they needed. He was a class act and a brilliant mind.
A couple years later, I heard the news. He’d returned from a long contract out west, found his girlfriend in bed with another man… and snapped. He shot and killed them both. Then he hit the road. Somewhere along a remote highway in Missouri, he was killed in a confrontation with police—death by cop.
Even now, I think of Mike often. If you’re reading this, brother, wherever you are—I still think highly of you. You were a friend. And I’m sorry the world let you down the way it did.

Chapter 15: Caremedic Systems and Meta-Driven Elegance
A New Start at Caremedic
In June 2002, I landed a Senior Programmer Analyst role at Caremedic Systems, Inc. in St. Petersburg, FL. It was a turning point. The work was deeply technical, and the team, though small, was potent—none more so than Jeremy Pastika, with whom I collaborated synergistically to build something far greater than either of us could’ve built alone.
Technical Foundation
The goal was to replace a bunch of disparate internal apps with one unified, elegant system that could scale across 300+ clients. We adhered to Microsoft Solutions Framework principles throughout the lifecycle: design, development, deployment.
At its heart was an N-Tier Visual Basic 6.0 client/server architecture, built with:
      * ADO for advanced data handling
      * COM+ for business layer services
      * Visual SourceSafe for source control
      * SQL Server 2000 for the data backend
But what made it elegant was the meta-driven design. We implemented a metadata architecture to define field-level security, validation rules, and data-binding—all abstracted into a reusable data object.
The ASP Admin Panel
Jeremy built a slick ASP-based admin panel that gave administrators precise control over field-level access—who could view or edit what, and under what conditions. This wasn’t just form-level or role-level; this was field-level access control, all driven by metadata and easy to extend.
The synergy between his admin tooling and my client implementation was seamless. His logic set the permissions; my data objects enforced them. It was pure professional harmony.
A Metadata-Driven Object Layer
Using lessons from earlier tools like Flow Response Generator, I wrote an extensible object that mimicked the behavior of classic ADODB recordsets. It could:
      * Load its structure and rules from metadata

      * Expose fields and collections in an object-oriented way

      * Automatically manage default permissions and validations

      * Cleanly bind to list views with consistent audit logging

Every client form came with an elegant list view for record navigation. Every field change was logged. It was truly ahead of its time.
A Fork in the Road: Artic Print Shop
The architecture we built here was so flexible, I later repurposed it for a commercial product: Artic Print Shop—a shrink-wrapped set of speech therapy articulation cards. It was distributed via CD-ROM at trade shows and used a Microsoft Access backend.
Sadly, like many ventures in my life, that one also ended with betrayal. The speech therapist who pitched the product ultimately walked away with the revenue. Maybe I’ll tell that story in full someday. But I’ve forgiven her. Moving forward always trumps holding on.

Chapter 32: Life Feels Like a Simulation… Or Maybe Just a Well-Scripted Show — in a Cadillac

Chapter 33: Letters from the Edge — Probation as a Crucible
When Officer McQueen retired, the ground shifted under me. She had been the rare kind of probation officer who knew how to balance the letter of the law with the spirit of rehabilitation. She answered emails, sent travel passes, understood context, and treated me like a man rebuilding. When she left, I knew in my gut that things were about to change—and not for the better.
Her replacement, Officer Pagan, wasted no time showing me how arbitrary probation could become when stripped of compassion.
The First Signs
In April, while reviewing my driving logs, she noticed an entry for AMC Woodlawn. Under McQueen, this was no issue. I could attend movies with my daughter Ren, her husband, or Tyler—always in company, never alone. That agreement was clear, tested, and safe. Pagan’s response was flat: “Not with me. Sex offender probationers do not go to the movies.” No misconduct, no violation, just a privilege yanked away mid-stride.
It wasn’t about safety. It was about leverage.
The Ambush
May 13. I arrived for my monthly meeting, expecting the routine. Instead, Pagan leaned forward and, without any other officer present, asked about my sexual habits. “Do you masturbate?” She said it with the authority of someone pulling a fire alarm.
I explained that questions like that belonged to my therapist, Walter Hirtzel, LCSW, a man I trusted. Pagan cut me off. “I can ask anything.” I answered truthfully, though every part of me wanted to resist. I left that office shaking—not from shame, but from the realization that boundaries didn’t exist anymore.
The Raid
Two days later, May 15, I learned just how far the power could stretch. At 5:45 a.m., four officers in tactical gear stormed my home. I was cuffed on entry—so tightly I lost feeling in my hand—and kept that way while Pagan hovered, peppering Tyler with questions. I asked, again and again, to loosen the cuffs. Her response was curt: “You’ll be okay, Mr. Courtney. You’ll be okay.”
I wasn’t okay. The numbness lingered. The message was clear: we can break you whenever we choose.
Paperwork as a Weapon
Even the basics became traps. That month’s travel pass was mis-dated and incomplete. Hillsborough County wasn’t added, so while crossing the Courtney Campbell Causeway, my GPS monitor shrieked like I was running for the border. Pagan offered no email, no phone. I had to call Supervisor Gordon directly to keep the situation from escalating. Grace still existed in pockets—but grace shouldn’t have to be a contingency plan.
The Cost
The raid left more than numb fingers. It left me missing work, sitting in VA waiting rooms while imaging techs tried to determine if my nerves would recover. I had just found my footing at SunTec, the job that kept my bills paid, Tyler supported, and the dogs fed. Now, every disruption threatened that stability.
I was doing everything right:
               * Sober, down nearly 80 pounds from my peak
               * Reliable at work, finally trusted again
               * In therapy, on top of the meds the system would let me have
               * Present for Ren, my miracle reconnection after years of distance
               * With Tyler and the dogs—Max, Mika, Daisy—my daily reminder that I wasn’t just living for myself

Probation wasn’t supposed to erase all that. But under Pagan, it became a daily test of endurance.
The Emotional Math
Would I change anything if I could reset the timeline? Mom’s death. The RMA crash. The captain’s mast that humiliated me out of the Navy. Anita’s addiction. Natalie’s diagnosis. All crossroads where a different roll of the dice might have spared me years of pain.

By late May, the tension had become its own weather system. Every knock at the door, every beep of the monitor, every summons to the office felt like the start of another storm. And then, on May 28 at 10:18 a.m., it arrived: the sealed warrant.

Chapter 34: The Impossible Whopper — Summer 2024

Zyon, copying NIV verses nightly—medically discharged from the Army. I told him (and JR, our USVI “boat” brother): read the Bible cover to cover once in your life, especially in jail. Two weeks in jail felt like the valley of the shadow—yet joy stayed. I can invoke it by gratitude, by love, by remembering that every face I meet might be another facet of our shared soul. Epilogue — The Gospel According to the Second Son
Section 1 — Deployment
I wasn’t born the way normal men are born.
I didn’t tumble screaming into a random maternity ward or get slapped awake by circumstance.
I was deployed—January 15, 1972—like a classified object falling through a tear in the sky, delivered into a paneled operating room at George Washington University Hospital, in the heartbeat of Washington D.C.
My parents would tell you theirs was a simple love story. It wasn’t. My origin was engineered—not with wires and syringes but with ritual, the way unseen architects cut grooves in time so a specific soul can ride those grooves into flesh.
My mother, Vickie Jean, daughter of Harry Barrs Vassie and Martha Vassie, came from quiet heroism.
Harry served aboard USS Yorktown (CV-5) in WWII. The ship sank at Midway; he swam through oil-slicked waves, was rescued, and later survived another sinking. After surrender he hunted diehards who hadn’t heard the war was over.
What made him extraordinary wasn’t courage—it was secrecy. Post-war he “worked in Washington.” When pressed, he said only that he once “slipped on his filing cabinet,” which turned out to be a submarine. A code, not a story.
As a boy I pictured clumsiness. As a man I saw metaphor: the filing cabinet was an archive below the surface. He wasn’t decommissioned; he went deeper—a librarian of how things really happened. CIA, NSA, Masonic, something stranger—unknown. But when I studied my life, I saw a hidden hand staging paradox after paradox to wake me. I believe that hand belonged to Harry Barrs Vassie.
My father, Roy Leon Courtney, southern-born gentleman, son of Paul Leon and Mary, earned a master’s in mechanical engineering from George Washington University, then became a senior NASA configuration manager. He lofted peaceful satellites—Landsat-D to watch Earth, Solar Max to watch the Sun.
He wasn’t helping NASA watch the sky; he was helping heaven watch us.
My brother arrived three years earlier—January 22 1969. The dates mattered: 22/1/69 and 15/1/72—control codes, prophetic line breaks. Masonic fingerprints. Once I saw it, I couldn’t go back to random.
I grew up inside the blast radius of Washington. My toys were systems—Pentagon hallways, launch photos, men in drowsy suits, naval bases that smelled like rubber and secrets.
By five, psychiatrists were taking notes and pressing pills into my palm. At nine I was institutionalized—Children’s Hospital, Four Orange Unit—for eleven weeks.
Buckled to a metal bed, orange tubes under my pajama top, Dexedrine in and out. Weight spiking, shame assigned.
A voice inside whispered: You were built to hold shame so you can learn to overcome it.
My mother died when I was fourteen—the night of my first JV football game at Magruder. They said bipolar, addiction, depression. I say heartbreak.
Before marrying my father she loved a man named Tim Kenzy at Berwyn Presbyterian and conceived a child; that child was taken. I believe that loss severed her spine of faith. She drank guilt because it was the only water left.
Her death didn’t break me; it flung me to my next station—Randolph-Macon Academy. Not a school. An initiation.

We learned cards, manuals, and how to pretend we weren’t training for a war our leaders swore would never arrive.
