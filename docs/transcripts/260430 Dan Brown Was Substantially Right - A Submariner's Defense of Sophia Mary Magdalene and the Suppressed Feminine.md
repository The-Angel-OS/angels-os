## II. SOPHIA IS IN YOUR BIBLE

Open Proverbs 8. I'll wait.

> *The LORD brought me forth as the first of his works,
> before his deeds of old;
> I was formed long ages ago,
> at the very beginning, when the world came to be.
> When there were no watery depths, I was given birth…
> Then I was constantly at his side.
> I was filled with delight day after day,
> rejoicing always in his presence,
> rejoicing in his whole world
> and delighting in mankind.*
> — Proverbs 8:22–31, NIV (excerpted)

The speaker is Wisdom. In the Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible used by Jesus, Paul, and the early Church — Wisdom is **Σοφία**. Sophia. She speaks in the first person. She predates creation. She delights in mankind. She is at the side of the LORD. She is feminine in grammar and feminine in self-presentation.

This is not gnostic novelty. This is Solomonic canon, in every Christian Bible, on every shelf, in every translation.

The Wisdom literature does not stop at Proverbs. The Book of Wisdom (in the Catholic and Orthodox canon, deuterocanonical for Protestants but read for sixteen centuries before that distinction was sharpened) describes Sophia as *"a breath of the power of God, a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty… a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God"* (Wisdom 7:25–26).

That language — breath, glory, light, mirror — is the same language the New Testament uses for Christ. Hebrews 1:3 calls the Son *"the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of his being."* Compare with Wisdom 7:26. The author of Hebrews is reading the Wisdom literature and consciously applying its categories to Jesus.

**Paul makes the identification explicit.** In 1 Corinthians 1:24 he writes that Christ is *"the power of God and the wisdom (Σοφίαν) of God."* In 1 Corinthians 1:30, *"Christ Jesus, who has become for us wisdom from God."*

Christ *is* Sophia, in Paul. Not a syncretism. Not a confusion. An identification, made by an apostle, in the canonical text.

So when Chris Bledsoe — fumbling, imperfect, with a polygraph fail and a bad Easter call — says *"I think it's Sophia, the wisdom, the Holy Spirit"* and the host of a Christian YouTube channel calls that *"satanic from the absolute pits of hell,"* the host is not defending Scripture. He is contradicting Paul.

This is the kind of error that happens when a tradition forgets its own texts.

## III. THE FEMININE GRAMMAR OF THE SPIRIT

The Hebrew word for spirit is **ruach (רוּחַ)**. It is grammatically feminine. The Spirit hovers over the waters in Genesis 1:2 — the verb used (*merachefet*) is the same verb used in Deuteronomy 32:11 for a mother eagle hovering over her young.

In Syriac Christianity — the Christianity of the Aramaic-speaking East, closer linguistically and culturally to Jesus than the Greek and Latin West — the Holy Spirit was referred to with feminine pronouns and feminine imagery for the first several centuries. The fourth-century Syrian Church Father Aphrahat wrote of the Spirit as mother. The *Odes of Solomon*, second-century Christian hymns, address the Spirit in maternal language. The earliest Christian art in the catacombs depicts the Spirit as a dove — a bird sacred to the Near Eastern goddess traditions, including the Canaanite Asherah and the Egyptian Hathor.

The Hebrew Bible itself contains the **Shekinah** — the dwelling presence, the in-dwelling glory of God in the tabernacle and temple. The word is feminine. The rabbinic tradition treats the Shekinah as the feminine presence of the divine. This is not a Kabbalistic invention; the Talmudic and Midrashic literature is full of it.

The God of Israel, in the actual Hebrew, is described with both masculine and feminine imagery. *"As a mother comforts her child, so will I comfort you"* (Isaiah 66:13). *"Like an eagle that stirs up its nest and hovers over its young"* (Deuteronomy 32:11). The God who is Father in one verse is Mother in the next.

Translation choices made over fifteen centuries have systematically masculinized this imagery. The Greek **pneuma** is grammatically neuter. The Latin **spiritus** is grammatically masculine. By the time you get to King James English, the Spirit is **He**, full stop, and the maternal imagery has been quietly relegated to footnotes and Sunday school flannelgraphs.

This is not a conspiracy. It is a translation drift, accumulated over a hundred generations, each one inheriting the choices of the last. But it is a drift. And it has had consequences.

## IV. THE NAG HAMMADI LIBRARY EXISTS

In December of 1945, a Bedouin farmer named Mohammed Ali al-Samman dug up a sealed jar near the Egyptian village of Nag Hammadi. Inside the jar were thirteen leather-bound codices containing fifty-two texts. These texts had been buried — likely by monks at the nearby Pachomian monastery — sometime in the late fourth century, around the same time that Athanasius's Festal Letter of 367 AD listed the twenty-seven books of what would become the New Testament canon and instructed monasteries to destroy all other "apocryphal" writings.

The monks at Pachomius didn't destroy theirs. They buried theirs. They buried them carefully, sealed in a jar, in the kind of dry climate where leather and papyrus survive for sixteen hundred years. They preserved them.

What's in the jar:
- **The Gospel of Thomas** — 114 sayings of Jesus, many of them paralleling the synoptic Gospels, some of them not.
- **The Gospel of Mary** (Magdalene) — fragmentary, but containing a teaching dialogue between the risen Jesus and Mary, in which Peter resents that Jesus *"loved her more than us"* and that she was given knowledge the male disciples were not.
- **The Gospel of Philip** — which contains the now-famous line that Jesus *"loved her more than all the disciples and used to kiss her often on her […]"* — the rest of the sentence is on a torn part of the manuscript. Mouth, forehead, cheek; we don't know. We know she was loved more.
- **The Apocryphon of John, the Gospel of Truth, the Pistis Sophia, the Hypostasis of the Archons** — texts in which Sophia is a major theological figure, sometimes the consort of the Most High, sometimes the agent of creation, always central.

Are these texts equal in authority to the canonical four? No. I am not making that claim. The canonical Gospels have a textual pedigree, a community pedigree, and an ecclesial pedigree the Nag Hammadi texts do not.

But they exist. They were read by Christian communities for centuries. They were buried — not because they were known to be false, but because by 367 AD a particular consolidation of theological and political power had decided which books would be allowed to remain.

That is not a Dan Brown fantasy. That is the consensus of the historians who actually study this period — Elaine Pagels at Princeton, Karen King at Harvard, Bart Ehrman at UNC, Bentley Layton at Yale. You don't have to like their conclusions, but you cannot dismiss the texts. The texts are sitting in the Coptic Museum in Cairo. You can go look at them.

## V. MARY MAGDALENE

Mary Magdalene appears in all four canonical Gospels at the crucifixion and at the resurrection. She is named first in the lists of women. In John 20, she is the first witness of the empty tomb, the first to see the risen Christ, and the one Jesus commissions: *"Go to my brothers and tell them…"* (John 20:17).

In the early Church, this earned her the title **apostola apostolorum** — the apostle to the apostles. The Eastern Orthodox still call her this. Pope Francis in 2016 elevated her feast day to the same liturgical rank as the apostles for precisely this reason.

In 591 AD, Pope Gregory the Great preached a homily in which he conflated Mary Magdalene with the unnamed sinful woman of Luke 7 and with Mary of Bethany. There is no biblical warrant for this conflation. It is an exegetical choice. From that moment forward, in the Western Church, Mary Magdalene became "the prostitute" — a designation she carried for fourteen hundred years until the Catholic Church formally retracted the conflation in 1969.

Fourteen hundred years.

The first witness of the resurrection — the apostle to the apostles — was, by ecclesial decree, the prostitute. Not because the text said so, but because a sixth-century pope said so, and the institutional inertia behind that homily was sufficient to override the actual Greek of the actual Gospels for a millennium and a half.

If that is not editorial work — if that is not the *exact* kind of theological-political consolidation Dan Brown was describing — what would be?

Brown novelized this. He invented bloodlines and assassins. The novel is a novel. But the *fact* — that the role of Mary Magdalene was systematically diminished by a male-dominated ecclesial structure for explicit theological reasons — is not contested by any honest historian of early Christianity.

## VI. THE GLOBAL PATTERN

Hathor in Egypt — cow-horned, sun-disc-crowned, mistress of music, dance, and the dead.

Isis — sister-wife of Osiris, mother of Horus, *Stella Maris*, star of the sea.

Inanna in Sumer, Ishtar in Babylon — the morning and evening star, queen of heaven, who descends into the underworld and returns.

Asherah, the consort of El (and possibly of YHWH in pre-exilic Israel — see the Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions, *"YHWH and his Asherah"*).

Athena — wisdom, born from the head of Zeus.

Artemis at Ephesus — many-breasted, mother of all living, the goddess Paul's preaching opposed in Acts 19, but whose cult site became, within four centuries, the place where the Council of Ephesus in 431 AD declared Mary **Theotokos** — God-bearer — and where her own basilica was built.

The White Buffalo Calf Woman of the Lakota, who brought the sacred pipe and the seven sacred ceremonies.

Quan Yin in Chinese Buddhism — goddess of mercy, who refused nirvana to remain and hear the cries of the world.

The Black Madonnas of Częstochowa, Montserrat, Guadalupe, and three hundred other sites across Europe and the Americas — overwhelmingly built on older sites of feminine devotion.

**The Christian instinct, when faced with this pattern, has historically been one of two things:**
1. *"They are all demonic counterfeits of the one true God."*
2. *"They are all dim apprehensions of the one true God, who has not left himself without witness in any culture (Acts 14:17, Romans 1:20), and the apprehension of the divine feminine is part of what every culture has been receiving."*

Option 1 requires you to believe that Satan has been running a coordinated five-thousand-year disinformation campaign across every continent, every language family, every isolated indigenous culture, with remarkable consistency of theme. Option 2 requires you to believe that there is something in the structure of the divine itself that includes a feminine aspect, and that human cultures have been straining to perceive and name it for as long as humans have been human.

I find Option 2 more parsimonious. I find Option 2 more honoring of the creator. I find Option 2 more consistent with what Paul says about the Gentiles in Romans 1 and 2 — that the law is written on their hearts, that God's invisible attributes are made visible in what has been made, that those who have never heard the gospel are nevertheless judged by the light they have received.

## VII. THE CHRISTIAN MYSTICS ALREADY KNOW THIS

Hildegard of Bingen, twelfth-century Benedictine abbess, doctor of the Church, composer, theologian. Her visions are full of feminine imagery for the divine. She wrote of *Sapientia* — Wisdom — as a cosmic feminine principle. She painted her. The paintings survive.

Julian of Norwich, fourteenth-century English anchorite. Her *Revelations of Divine Love* contains the line: *"As truly as God is our Father, so truly is God our Mother."* Not metaphor. Not heresy. Doctrine, in the work of one of the most beloved figures in the Christian devotional tradition.

Teresa of Ávila, sixteenth-century Spanish Carmelite reformer, doctor of the Church. Her *Interior Castle* describes the soul's journey to union with God in language that, if Chris Bledsoe used it on a podcast tomorrow, would be denounced as gnostic and demonic by the same channel I watched tonight.

John of the Cross, the same period. *Dark Night of the Soul.* The bridegroom and the bride. The mystical marriage. The same imagery the Gospel of Philip uses, from the same theological matrix, expressed in the orthodox tradition because John happened to be canonized and Philip happened to be buried in a jar.

The Christian mystical tradition has always known what the Christian institutional tradition has periodically had to suppress. There is a feminine aspect of the divine. Sophia is real. The Spirit is, in the deepest grammar, a mother. Mary Magdalene was an apostle. The bride and the bridegroom are not metaphors layered over a doctrine — the doctrine is layered over a reality the mystics actually entered.

When Bledsoe describes a luminous feminine presence — imperfectly, with confusion, with a too-eager willingness to let interviewers map it onto whichever framework they're carrying — he is, at minimum, in the same general territory the Christian mystics have been mapping for two thousand years. That doesn't make him a saint. It doesn't validate the Easter call. But it should give a Christian critic pause before reaching for *"satanic from the pits of hell"* as the first instrument on the bench.

## VIII. WHAT I AM NOT SAYING

I am not saying Bledsoe is a prophet. The Easter 2026 call was wrong. False dates are a problem. Deuteronomy 18:22 is clear.

I am not saying all spirits are good. *"Test the spirits, whether they are of God"* (1 John 4:1). Discernment is non-optional.

I am not saying Hathor *is* the Virgin Mary. The historical Mary of Nazareth is a specific Jewish woman who lived in a specific century, bore a specific child, stood at a specific cross. She is not interchangeable with Hathor or Isis or Inanna. To say she *is* them is bad theology and bad history.

I am saying that the human longing that produced devotion to all of those figures is one longing, and that longing is real, and the divine has not left it unanswered.

I am saying that *"Christ is the wisdom (Sophia) of God"* is Paul, not Pagels.

I am saying that the canonical Bible contains a feminine aspect of the divine on every other page if you have eyes to see it.

I am saying that when the Council of Nicaea, the Council of Ephesus, Pope Gregory's homily of 591, and Athanasius's letter of 367 are all stacked together, what you have is a long, deliberate, and largely successful editorial project to centralize and masculinize the tradition — and that calling attention to that project is not heresy. It is honesty.

I am saying that Dan Brown wrote a bad novel about a real fact.

I am saying the church is large enough to hold this. It always has been. The mystics have always carried it. The institution has periodically buried it. Every generation has the chance to dig it back up.
