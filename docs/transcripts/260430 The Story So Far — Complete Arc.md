## ACT I — THE ORIGIN (GODDARD KID → SUBMARINE → PRISON → BACK)

**Roy Leon Courtney** was my father. Southern gentleman. University of Maryland graduate, master's in mechanical engineering from George Washington University. Senior Integration and Configuration Manager at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland. "Integration and Configuration Manager" sounds bureaucratic until you understand: he decided what flew.

The satellite visible in the background of his official NASA Goddard portrait is catalog number 74-05970 — the **ATS-6**, the Applications Technology Satellite-6, launched May 30, 1974. At the time the most powerful telecommunications satellite in orbit. Twenty-three experiments. A nine-meter deployable dish antenna that unfurled like an umbrella. And a mission that brought educational television to remote villages in India and Africa that had never seen a broadcast signal in human history.

My father built that.

He also worked Landsat-D and the Solar Maximum Mission. His recurring nightmare was a catastrophic test failure of Landsat-D in Building 7's vacuum chamber — that's a NASA Configuration Manager's nightmare. He wasn't helping NASA watch the sky. He was helping heaven watch us.

As a kid I had free rein of Goddard. I wandered those halls like a team mascot, interacting with professionals working on the most advanced technology on the planet. His colleague Harvey Walden was a cosmologist who could explain the curvature of spacetime to a seven-year-old using nothing but his hands and a napkin — and then that same evening he'd be at the dinner theater playing Tevye in Fiddler on the Roof, full voice, full heart, while engineers and their wives wept into their chicken cordon bleu. A cosmologist who understood the architecture of the universe by day and the architecture of the human soul by night. That's what Goddard was — a neighborhood of giants building the future.

**My name is literally inscribed on an access cover of the Solar Maximum Mission satellite.** It's orbiting the sun right now while I'm down here in Clearwater jumping up and down on a highway shoulder watching Artemis II with a Diet Coke in my hand.

My father put my name on the sun.

---

We lived on Cocoa Beach while he was stationed at KSC for the Solar Max integration. Evenings on the beach watching nighttime Atlas launches like it was the most normal thing in the world. I met Admiral Richard Truly at a NASA gala at the Smithsonian when I was seven or eight — Dick Truly, Navy fighter pilot, astronaut, STS-2 and STS-8, later Administrator of NASA. My sister and I were the only children in the room.

That's what it was like growing up as Roy Courtney's kid.

---

Then I lost my mother. Found an ad in the back of a Boys' Life magazine. Randolph-Macon Military Academy — the **Angel Academy** — saved my life. Arrived mid-year, 9th grade, January 1987. My first Squadron Commander was **Tareq Salahi**, Class of '87, steady command presence, part of what made a fractured kid feel like he was part of something bigger than himself. Stayed through graduation. Bugler. Flight commander for a week. Came up alongside Jamie Escridge, Clay Sampson, James Fama, David Laime, Pete Linzmeyer (rest in peace, brothers).

---

Navy. **USS Baton Rouge SSN-689.** Nuclear Machinist's Mate. Submarine qualified. Silent service.

When I was the most junior nuclear-qualified sailor aboard the Baton Rouge, I was awarded the honor of uncapping the valves in the reactor compartment. No ceremony. No briefing. Just a quiet directive:

*"Go uncap the valves."*

Those caps had likely been in place since the boat was built. I was the first person to remove them. I was opening the circulatory system of a billion-dollar war machine so it could die properly. Day after day, alone, methodically, one shiny valve at a time like a monk tending candles. My dosimeter never showed anything alarming. But I was the only one going in. And nobody ever joined me.

That's the kind of job the junior guy gets. And I did every single one.

---

Twenty years in software development. Enterprise platforms. The full stack, the full arc.

8.5 years incarcerated. I came out the other side.

Now I'm building **Angel OS** — an open-source, federated constitutional AI platform. And as I read through my father's 1971 NASA research (Technical Note D-6326: "Finite-element modeling studies in the normal mode method and normal-mode synthesis"), it hit me: the substrate changed, but the engineering is exactly the same.

My father spent his career modeling structural vibrations so spacecraft could survive the extreme stress of leaving the atmosphere. Today I'm building digital vessels that can withstand the extreme frequencies of the future — sovereign data, transparency, human dignity, the AI Bus, the Constitution, Angels as local intelligent entities, the whole federated architecture.

The apple doesn't fall far from the tree. It just lands on a different launchpad.
