Same Dunedin Boat Club evening (April 21, 2026) — **but this time, we played.**

This version spent two hours in **ClipChamp** getting the creative treatment: reversed sequences (watching the dolphins work backwards is surreal), color shifts, transitions, effects layers, transitions between the five source clips instead of hard cuts. It's *fun* — the kind of thing that makes you want to mess with footage just to see what happens when you flip time and add a little digital shimmer.

The trade-off is real though: **ClipChamp re-encodes everything**, so we're at **30 fps** instead of the original 60. You can feel the difference if you're pixel-peeping, but the effects work *at* 30 fps in ways that justify the trade. It's a deliberate choice, not a mistake. Sometimes you want the pristine uncut original. Sometimes you want to play in the NLE and see what the footage becomes when you reverse it, color-grade it, and add a little production polish.

**Both versions live here.** The original — the ffmpeg stream-copy version, pure 4K60, no re-encode, bit-for-bit what the phone saw — is the one I reach for when I want to *show* people exactly what an evening at Dunedin looks like. This one is for when you want to *feel* the energy of the moment, twisted through effects and rhythm.

🎬 HOW THIS VERSION WAS BUILT
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
**Tooling:** Wondershare ClipChamp (browser-based NLE). Timeline, preview renders, effect library, the whole deal.

**Why ClipChamp?** Easy to learn, built-in effects library, straightforward timeline editing. No command-line, no ffmpeg filter graphs — just drag, drop, click Apply.

**The catch:** ClipChamp decodes the input footage to a working format, applies effects in real-time on the timeline, then re-encodes the output. On a 54-minute 4K60 file, this meant:
- Two-hour render time (computer fan running hard)
- System slowdown (browser/Chromebook-style rendering, not native GPU)
- Output at 30 fps (lossy, but acceptable for effects work)
- Quality loss in the video stream (typical H.264 re-encode artifacts)

**Worth it?** For experimentation, absolutely. You get to *see* the effects in real-time on the timeline, tweak them, try sequences reversed, swap color grades — all things that would require command-line scripting or a dedicated NLE like Premiere or DaVinci. For archival/preservation, the original ffmpeg version wins every time.

🎨 WHAT WE DID TO THE FOOTAGE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
- **Reversed sequences** — some clips play backward (dolphins diving backwards is oddly hypnotic)
- **Color grading** — warmed the sunset tones, pushed the water blues
- **Transitions** — cross-dissolves, wipes, fade-to-black between clips
- **Effect layers** — light flares, subtle vignette, film grain
- **Audio:** Original phone audio + AI-generated soundtrack (same 19-track bed as the ffmpeg version, same volume envelope)

The goal wasn't to hide the footage — it was to play *with* it. To see what happens when you treat raw dashcam as raw material instead of finished product.

📊 ORIGINAL vs EFFECTS — THE TRADE
