# My Father Built the Satellite That Brought Television to Africa. Fifty Years Later, I Watched Artemis II From the Roadside With a Diet Coke.

*This started as a comment on Ellie in Space's breakdown of the third New Glenn launch. YouTube's spam filter apparently decided a Stephen King novel didn't belong in a comment thread, which is fair. So I'm giving it a permanent home here, where my former RMA classmates, my old Advantage International colleagues, the NASA Goddard families whose parents worked alongside mine, and the aerospace community at large can actually find it.*

I want to tell you about three Ellies, one satellite, one father, and one roadside moment in Florida where I finally understood everything.

Three Ellies.

Ellie Arroway in Contact who wouldn't stop listening until the universe answered back. Ellie in Deep Impact who chased the truth while the sky was literally falling. And now Ellie in Space — Eliana Sheriff — who walked away from eight years in television news to chase rockets full time because the story was too important to leave to anyone else. Three Ellies, same fire, all of them worth your time.

Who I am.

I'm Kenneth Scott Courtney. I run a tiny channel out of Clearwater, Florida called Clearwater Cruisin' — 96 subscribers, three dogs named Daisy, Mika, and Max, a 4K 60fps gimbal, and a daily Soul Quest that takes us from Enterprise Dog Park to the Dunedin Sailing Club Marina where dolphins surface within feet of the docks so consistently that our catchphrase has become "We Find Dolphins So You Don't Have To." Max we think of as Falcor from The Neverending Story — he's aging in reverse, pure Benjamin Button energy. I'm a Navy veteran, USS Baton Rouge SSN-689, Nuclear Machinist's Mate, submarine qualified, silent service. Twenty-year software developer now building something called Angel OS. Different universe than what Ellie covers but built from the same love of the frontier.

The rocket part.

The third New Glenn launch put AST SpaceMobile's Bluebird 7 satellite into the wrong orbit. Two out of three New Glenn flights now have had upper stage issues. Meanwhile SpaceX casually drops its 600th booster landing like it's a Tuesday morning in Boca Chica. Ellie's coverage of the tension between Blue Origin's conservative, methodical approach and SpaceX's iterative test-it-until-it-breaks philosophy is the clearest analysis anywhere on YouTube.

I've been out to the Cape multiple times and space content is woven into the DNA of my little channel the same way it's woven into my actual DNA. I watched a New Glenn attempt solo from Jetty Park and got great dashcam footage. Tyler and I drove out for Artemis II on April 1st — Courtney Campbell Causeway out of Clearwater through Malfunction Junction to the Beeline down SR 528 — and caught the launch just before we hit the Beeline, then kept driving because my best buddy owns Space Coast Cactus Farm, formerly Hayes Cactus Farm, about a mile from the entrance to the base, and we watch launches from on top of his fifth wheel trailer like a private VIP platform surrounded by succulents. On March 2nd we covered a Starlink launch from the Ascendance cruise and the footage is something else — there's something about watching a Falcon 9 climb out of the Atlantic from the deck of a ship that makes you feel like you're living inside a science fiction novel you haven't written yet. And on another Cape trip I ended up at the Sandbar Sports Grill on Cocoa Beach — the legendary spot right where 520 meets the beach, behind Ron Jon's, where astronauts and engineers have been drinking since before either of us was born — and fell into an incredible evening with a group of Boeing engineers out there for launch support. Hours of real talk about engineering culture differences between programs.

But the rocket part isn't why I'm writing this.

Here's why I ugly-cry every time something leaves the pad.

My father was Roy Leon Courtney. Southern gentleman, University of Maryland graduate, master's in mechanical engineering from George Washington University. He was a senior Integration and Configuration Manager at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland — which sounds like a bureaucratic title until you understand what it means. He was the final authority on what made it onto the satellite. Testing, packaging, shipment to the launch facility, and a role in launch control. He decided what flew.

I recently identified the satellite visible in the background of his official NASA Goddard portrait — catalog number 74-05970 — and confirmed it's the ATS-6, the Applications Technology Satellite-6, launched May 30, 1974\. At the time it was the most powerful telecommunications satellite in orbit. Twenty-three experiments. A nine-meter deployable dish antenna that unfurled like an umbrella. And a mission that brought educational television to remote villages in India and Africa that had never seen a broadcast signal in human history.

My father built that.

He was Mufasa — quiet authority, total competence, love expressed through engineering that changed the world from a desk in Greenbelt, Maryland.

He also worked Landsat-D and the Solar Maximum Mission. His recurring nightmare wasn't about money or relationships — it was about a catastrophic test failure of Landsat-D in Building 7's vacuum chamber where the satellite crashes from its test harness and gets destroyed. That's a NASA Configuration Manager's nightmare. He wasn't helping NASA watch the sky. He was helping heaven watch us.

The neighborhood of giants.

As a kid I had free rein of Goddard. My dad was a single parent for a stretch and I wandered those halls like a team mascot, interacting with professionals working on the most advanced technology on the planet. When I had physics questions he'd get this look on his face — half proud, half amused — and say he was "just an engineer." Then he'd walk me down the hall to his colleague Harvey Walden.

Harvey was a cosmologist who worked at Goddard for decades and might still be there for all I know. He could explain the curvature of spacetime to a seven-year-old using nothing but his hands and a napkin and make you feel like you almost understood it, and then that same evening he'd be at the dinner theater near the center playing Tevye in Fiddler on the Roof — full voice, full heart — belting out "If I Were a Rich Man" to a crowd of engineers and their wives who had spent the day calculating orbital insertion burns and were now weeping into their chicken cordon bleu because Harvey Walden as Tevye could do that to a person.

A cosmologist who understood the architecture of the universe by day and the architecture of the human soul by night. That's the kind of place Goddard was — a neighborhood of giants who happened to be building the future.

My name on the sun.

My dad brought home exotic alloy satellite components that had failed testing and they looked like alien artifacts to a kid in suburban Maryland. I wish I still had one. He kept an unabridged Oxford Dictionary on his desk and would read through the entire thing, marking each complete pass with a unique glyph. Multiple complete passes. That's the kind of man he was.

And my name is literally inscribed on an access cover of the Solar Maximum Mission satellite.

It's up there right now, orbiting the sun, while I'm down here in Clearwater jumping up and down on the side of the road watching Artemis II with a Diet Coke in my hand.

My father put my name on that bird. He put my name on the sun.

Cocoa Beach, second grade, Capeview Elementary.

When we lived on Cocoa Beach while he was stationed at KSC for the Solar Max integration, he gave our whole family a personalized insider tour of Kennedy Space Center. The bunkhouses where he'd point to the seat he manned during launch. The vehicle integration room where they were mounting Solar Max onto the launch vehicle. The Saturn V crawlers up close. Evenings on the beach watching nighttime Atlas launches like it was the most normal thing in the world.

I met Admiral Richard Truly at a NASA celebration gala at the Smithsonian when I was about seven or eight. Dick Truly — Navy fighter pilot, astronaut, STS-2 and STS-8, later Administrator of NASA. My sister and I were the only children in the room. I have a photo somewhere of us in little suits standing next to a man who would go on to run the entire agency.

That's what it was like growing up as Roy Courtney's kid.

April 1st, 2026\.

So when I pulled that truck over on April 1st and jumped out and literally jumped up and down in traffic pointing at the sky with Tim Dodd's livestream blasting from the speakers while the actual Artemis II launch boomed in my other ear and Tyler filming from the van window and a Diet Coke sweating in my hand — I wasn't just watching a rocket.

I was watching my father's legacy climb into the Florida sky.

Men going further from Earth than anyone since 1972\.

I ugly-cried on camera and I don't care who knows it. The footage is on my channel — a one-minute reaction Short and a full road trip video that captures what it actually feels like to be a regular person standing on the shoulder of a Florida highway watching history happen with the person you love.
