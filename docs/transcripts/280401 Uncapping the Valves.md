Uncapping the Valves — The Quietest, Heaviest Job I Ever Had  
There’s a job on a submarine that nobody talks about because it sounds boring, and because the people who do it rarely realize how significant it is while they’re doing it. For me, that job was uncapping the valves in the reactor compartment.  
As the most junior nuclear‑qualified sailor aboard the Baton Rouge, I was “awarded” the honor. No ceremony. No briefing. Just a quiet directive passed down the chain:  
“Go uncap the valves.”  
I didn’t know then what I know now — that those caps had likely been in place since the boat was built. That I was the first person to remove them. That I was, in a strange way, opening up the circulatory system of a billion‑dollar war machine so it could die properly.  
At the time, it just felt like another job the new guy gets stuck with.  
The reactor compartment was a controlled space, and I went in alone, day after day, working methodically one shiny valve at a time like a monk tending candles. Each cap had to be removed carefully, methodically, without dropping hardware, without damaging threads, without losing track of what had been touched and what hadn’t. Place a radiation containment bag over it. Seal it with tape. Grasp the relief valve, twist counterclose? Apply additional torque if necessary with a wrench. Into the bag, gradually releasing torque if there’s any pressure on the other side. It was slow work. Quiet work. The kind of work that gives your mind too much room to wander.  
My dosimeter never showed anything alarming.  
But I was the only one going in.  
And nobody ever joined me.  
Looking back, I can see the humor in it — the whole crew playing along, letting the new guy march into the potentially higher‑radiation space because someone had to do it and tradition dictated who that someone was. No malice. No conspiracy. Just the unspoken logic of submarine life:  
If the job sucks, give it to the junior guy.  
And I did it.  
All of it.  
Every cap.  
Every valve.  
Every day until the job was done.  
I didn’t realize at the time how rare that was — how few people ever get to say they uncapped the valves of a Los Angeles‑class submarine, especially one with the history the Baton Rouge carried. I didn’t realize that I was, in a small way, part of the boat’s final rites.  
All I knew was that I took pride in it.  
That it felt like a responsibility.  
That it mattered, even if nobody said so.  
And I lived to tell the tale — not because I’m made of anything mythical, but because sometimes the Navy hands you a job that sounds like nothing and turns out to be something you carry with you for the rest of your life.
