## Two things I need from you

Everything below this line is guesswork until these land.

### 1. Landed cost per unit

Not the sell price — what a belt costs you, delivered. Two figures if you have
them: **what the 2,500 cost**, and **what the next 2,500 would cost**.

I'm not asking what you make. I'm asking what I'm allowed to spend to win a
customer, and that number comes out of cost, not price. Without it, any ROI
figure I hand you is invented — and invented figures are exactly what you said
you're tired of.

### 2. The claims list

What you'll stand behind, in writing. Three buckets:

- **Will stand behind** — you have something to point at
- **Believe, can't document** — true in your experience, no paper
- **Never say** — off the table regardless

Plus, when you can: **what the manufacturer's instructions-for-use actually
state.** That document defines the product's intended use, and it's the line
that decides what the site can say.

**On incontinence.** It's a bigger market than fat loss, it's far less crowded,
and it doesn't need the before-and-after photos that Meta bans outright. I want
to lead with it. That's precisely why it needs to sit on something solid — the
stronger the claim, the more it's worth protecting.

### And a spec sheet, when convenient

Wavelengths, irradiance, treatment area, EMS channels and frequency, session
length, certifications, warranty terms.

This is the direct answer to your own point about comparing a Ferrari to a
tricycle. Nobody knows because the page has adjectives where it needs numbers.
A $99 belt and a $599 Kessela look identical in a search result — a spec table
is the only thing that separates them, and it's cheap to build once the numbers
exist.

If the specs come back *not* dramatically better than the cheap belts, that's
worth knowing in week one rather than after the ad spend.

## Pricing

What you told me, which is better data than most businesses have:

- $599 list, and **people don't balk at it**
- But: *"I doubt we'll get that"*
- **Three walk-ins bought three systems at $500 cash** — you discounted 17% on
  the spot, in person, unprompted

The honest read: **$599 holds face to face**, where the belt gets demonstrated
and the story gets told. It has never been tested cold, online, to a stranger.
So the job isn't a lower number — it's making a cold buyer as confident as that
walk-in was.

1. **Hold $599 and sell the reason.** Add to the offer rather than cutting the
   price — extended warranty, the studies, a setup call.
2. **Financing.** Affirm or Shop Pay turns a $599 decision into a ~$50/month
   one. Your current site already carries an Affirm badge.
3. **Multi-packs.** You discovered this yourself — the walk-ins bought *three*.
   This is a household and a friend-group purchase.
4. **A guarantee that closes.** You already have 14-day money-back and a
   warranty. Cold buyers need that stated at the price, not buried in a footer.

## Ad spend, and how you'll know it worked

You said you need an ROI, and that anybody can throw darts at a board. Agreed.
So this is a **measurement commitment, not a projection**:

- Every visit attributed to where it came from — already running
- Every sale traceable to its source
- One number you can look at without asking anyone: units, revenue, cost per
  customer
- **A stop rule, written before a dollar is spent.** If cost per customer
  exceeds the ceiling your landed cost sets, spend halts

The answer to "what's the ROI" is: *you'll know inside a week, and here's the
number that proves me wrong.* That's worth more than a forecast, and unlike a
forecast it's checkable.

**Channel order:** word of mouth first — it's already working and it costs
nothing — then paid. No trade shows.

**Two constraints on creative**, so nothing gets built that can't run: Meta
prohibits before-and-after and body-transformation ads. Google gates
medical-device advertising. Both shape the creative before the budget matters.
