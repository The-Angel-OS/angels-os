## 2. THE BURIED LEAD — this is not a fat-loss product

> *"Do you know what women's incontinence is? Urinary incontinence? … **this
> corrects.** … I'll put you on the conference call with Stephanie, who is the
> primary person that's been involved with this. **That's why I got 2,500.**"*
> — 01:15

He bought 2,500 units because of **urinary incontinence**, not body contouring.
Everything currently on kessela.com — fat cells, inches, "equal to 300 sit-ups"
— is the *wrong product story*, and it is the weaker of the two markets:

| | Body contouring | Pelvic floor / incontinence |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | discretionary, price-sensitive, seasonal | **urgent, recurring, under-served** |
| Competition | saturated, every gadget claims it | fewer consumer options |
| Ad platforms | **Meta bans before/after outright** | no before/after needed |
| Word of mouth | weak | **strong** — this is what Stephanie is |

**⚠️ And it is the bigger compliance risk, by a lot.** Treating urinary
incontinence is a **medical claim**. Kessela is a **registered** Class II device
— registered is a listing, *not* clearance and *not* approval. FDA-cleared
pelvic-floor stimulators exist; being one of them is a different regulatory
posture than being listed. **Do not put an incontinence claim on the site until
David says in writing what he'll stand behind and on what basis.**

This is the single most important thing to raise with David and Stephanie, and
the right framing is commercial, not legal: *the incontinence story is worth far
more than the fat story, which is exactly why it has to be built on something
that holds.*

## 3. The five points he demanded

> *"I need to understand what the pricing architecture is. What kind of spending?
> What is the assurance of an ROI? What's your strategy? … we're comparing a
> Ferrari to a tricycle, nobody knows. **Those are the five points I need to
> know.**"* — 04:25–04:51

This is the deliverable. Answer these five, in this order, in one page.

### 3.1 Pricing architecture

Real data he gave, unprompted:

- $599 list. *"When people buy it, they don't balk at the price."*
- But: *"I doubt we'll get that."*
- **Three walk-ins bought three systems at $500 cash each** — he discounted 17%
  on the spot, in person, without being pushed.

So the honest read: $599 holds face-to-face, where the belt can be demonstrated
and Stephanie's story can be told. It has never been tested cold, online, to a
stranger. **The architecture has to make the cold buyer as confident as the
walk-in was**, which is a job for financing, a guarantee, and proof — not for a
lower number.

Structure to model out (with the maths, per tier, at 2,500 units):

1. **Anchor at $599, sell the reason.** Never discount the number; add to the
   offer instead (extra warranty, the studies, a coaching call).
2. **Financing.** Affirm/Shop Pay at ~$50/mo reframes a $599 decision as a
   monthly one. Their existing site already carries an Affirm badge.
3. **Two/three-packs.** He already discovered this himself — the walk-ins bought
   *three*. Incontinence is a household and a friend-group conversation.
4. **A guarantee that does the closing.** He already has 14-day money-back and a
   warranty. Cold buyers need it stated at the price, not in a footer.

**Recommendation to carry into the call: hold $599, add financing, add a
3-pack.** The $500 cash sale is evidence of *willingness to buy*, not evidence
that $599 is wrong.

### 3.2 Ad spend — the condition of approval

He was explicit that he doesn't have numbers yet and neither do we, and equally
explicit that this is what the approval hangs on. **Do not invent a CAC.**

The honest structure:

- **No trade shows.** *"We're not going to trade shows anymore."*
- **Test budget, not a campaign.** A named small number over a named short
  window, with a kill rule written before it's spent.
- **What has to be true**: at $599 with ~$X landed cost, the gross margin per
  unit sets the maximum allowable CAC. **Get the landed unit cost from David —
  without it every ROI number is fiction.** ← *this is the one number the whole
  deliverable depends on.*
- **Channel order**: organic/word-of-mouth from Stephanie's network first
  (zero CAC, and it's the channel that's already working), then paid.
- Meta prohibits before/after and body-transformation ads; Google gates
  medical-device ads. This constrains creative before a dollar is spent.

### 3.3 ROI assurance

> *"All I know is I need a fucking ROI."* — 07:52

He is not asking for a forecast. He is asking to not be sold a dartboard —
*"anybody can throw dartboards against the board."* So the deliverable is a
**measurement promise, not a projection**:

- Every visit attributed to first touch (already built and running).
- Every sale traceable to its source.
- A single number he can look at without asking anyone: units, revenue, CAC.
- A **stop rule**: if CAC exceeds $X after N units, spend halts automatically.

**The ROI answer is "you will know, within a week, and here's the number that
makes me wrong."** That is more credible to him than any projection, and it
happens to be true.

### 3.4 Strategy

- **Lead with incontinence and Stephanie**, subject to §2.
- **The studies are the funnel** — 8 of them are live at `/posts` as of today.
  Someone spending $599 on a medical device reads before they buy.
- **The site can take money**; kessela.com cannot. That gap is the whole
  short-term revenue story.
- **Own the customer.** *"Your data needs to live somewhere and that source of
  truth should be you."* He accepted this framing on the call.

### 3.5 Positioning — "a Ferrari to a tricycle, nobody knows"

His actual complaint: **the product is materially better than what it's compared
to, and nothing on the page communicates that.** A $99 Amazon red-light belt and
a $599 Kessela look identical in a search result.

Fix in this order:
1. **A spec comparison** — irradiance, wavelengths, EMS channels, coverage area,
   duty cycle, vs. the cheap belts. Numbers, in a table. *(Needs David's spec
   sheet.)*
2. **Trust row** — FDA-registered, BBB, 14-day money back, warranty.
3. **Stephanie**, in her own words, on video.
